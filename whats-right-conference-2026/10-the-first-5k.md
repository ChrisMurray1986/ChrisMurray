# Build-Out #10 — "The First 5K"
## A stair-step on-ramp for leaders overwhelmed by the 2030 end state — with the touch-and-feel interactives

**The problem this solves:** most Rev Cycle leaders in the room don't know where to start. The 2030 end state reads like being told to run a marathon when they've never run a 5K. The archetype roadmaps (`archetyperoadmaps.md`) are the real sequencing — but they're written for our side of the table. The simulation insights are real — but they'd sail over the head of a leader who hasn't yet turned on a single agent. This build-out translates both into the room's language and gives them stairs they can actually climb.

**Companion assets:** the archetype roadmaps become the styled *leave-behind* (not stage content); the Bridge Builder app serves the further-along rooms; the Lab (`08`) remains the deep interactive for operationally mature audiences. This is the on-ramp *before* all of that.

---

## 0. The design principles (why this will land where the deck version wouldn't)

1. **They already know how to do this.** Every mechanic in the 2030 operating model is something a rev cycle leader already runs under another name. The session's job is not to teach new concepts — it's to *rename familiar ones*. This is the single most important framing decision: it converts fear ("I don't understand AI") into competence ("oh — that's just my QA program pointed at a computer").
2. **The end state is the direction, not the assignment.** Say it explicitly: *"Nobody is asking you to run the marathon. Your assignment is the next stair. The 2030 picture only exists so you don't build this quarter's stair facing the wrong way."*
3. **Stair 1 costs nothing and requires no permission.** The first moves are a pencil, a spreadsheet column, and a meeting they already hold. If the first stair requires a business case, we've failed.
4. **The first three stairs are the same on every road.** The archetype fork (Epic-forward vs. best-of-breed vs. build-the-edges) doesn't matter until stair 4 — which means the paralyzing question "which strategy do we pick?" can be *deferred with permission*. That's an enormous relief to say out loud.
5. **Nothing goes on a slide that a floor supervisor wouldn't say.** The simulation's insights come in — but translated into house rules (§4). If a sentence needs the word "verifiability," "orchestration," or "golden set," it gets rewritten until it doesn't.

## 1. The Rosetta Stone — the translation dictionary (print it; it's half the battle)

| The term we use | What it actually is | What they already have that it grows from |
|---|---|---|
| An **agent** | A new hire who reads fast, types fast, never sleeps — and needs exactly the supervision you'd give a temp in week one | Every new hire they've ever onboarded |
| **Observation mode** | The shadowing period: the new hire watches and suggests; a person still does the work | Their new-hire orientation and preceptor model |
| A **golden set / answer key** | The 50 cases your best person graded, kept as the standard to test anyone (or anything) against | Their QA program's graded cases — they already have this, they just don't save it |
| The **autonomy ladder (A1→A3 / T1→T3)** | How you already promote people: watch → do it with review → work independently with spot-checks. Computers climb the same ladder, and they have to earn each rung | Their training sign-off checklist |
| The **human floor / decision floors** | The signature matrix: what always requires a supervisor's signature, no matter how experienced the employee | Their write-off approval matrix — it already exists in policy |
| **Prospective capture ("turn on the recording")** | Two new columns on the denial log: who owned it, and did the appeal win | The denial log they already keep |
| The **autonomy policy / governance** | One page of house rules: what the computer may do alone, what needs a signature, and who can hit pause | Their existing P&P binder — this is one more page in it |
| An **error budget** | The QA threshold that triggers retraining — applied to the computer instead of a person | Their QA scorecard and corrective-action process |
| The **hand-back path** | The escalation tree: what happens when the new hire gets stuck | Their existing escalation matrix |
| The **command center** | The daily huddle board, live instead of monthly | The huddle they already run every morning |
| **Standardize first ("you can't automate a mess")** | The robot photocopies your process. Make sure it's photocopying the good version | Their SOP binder — the one that's three years stale, which is the point |
| **Licensed / lit / configured-to-depth** | The garage check: owning the treadmill ≠ plugging it in ≠ actually running on it | Every Epic module they bought and never fully turned on |

**Stage line that carries the whole section:** *"You already run every one of these. You call them QA, onboarding, the signature matrix, and the huddle. The 2030 operating model is your existing management system pointed at a different kind of worker."*

## 2. The Staircase — Couch to 5K to marathon, in their calendar

Present as five stairs. Each stair: what you do, what it costs, where it lives, what your team sees, and the proof you're ready for the next stair. Stairs 1–3 are archetype-independent (say so — it defers the strategy anxiety).

### STAIR 1 — "Write it down and start the tape" *(this quarter · ~$0 · no permission needed)*
- **What you do:** Pick ONE process (denials is the usual). Write the SOP as it's *actually worked* — not the 2019 binder version. Add **two columns** to the denial log: who owned the root cause, and did the appeal win. Have your best analyst grade 50 closed cases and *save the graded set* — that's your answer key. Name one owner for all of it (a person, not a committee).
- **Where it lives:** the denial committee meeting you already hold. No new meeting.
- **What the team sees:** nothing scary — a documentation sprint and two spreadsheet columns. Tell them why anyway: "we're getting our books in order."
- **Ready for Stair 2 when:** a stranger could work your process from the SOP, and the two columns have 90 days of real entries.
- **Why it can't wait (the one urgent thing):** the tape can't be rewound. Data you don't record this year cannot be created later — it's the only move on the whole staircase with a deadline.

### STAIR 2 — "The signature matrix and the garage check" *(next quarter · ~$0–low)*
- **What you do:** Write the one-page house rules: what a computer may do alone, what needs a human signature (your existing write-off matrix, extended), who can hit pause. Then the **garage check**: list every automation/AI module you already license in Epic, and mark each one honestly — *owned / plugged in / actually tuned*. Most "we have that" is "we own the license."
- **Where it lives:** one agenda item at your existing compliance touchpoint; the inventory is a two-week analyst project.
- **What the team sees:** leadership being deliberate before anything changes. That builds trust you'll spend later.
- **Ready for Stair 3 when:** the house-rules page is signed, and you have a ranked list of modules you own but never tuned — that list is your entire near-term backlog, and it's already paid for.

### STAIR 3 — "First helper on a leash" *(quarters 3–4 · first small spend)*
- **What you do:** Turn on ONE helper for ONE hated, checkable task — payer status checks, eligibility depth, a filing-deadline alarm. Run it in **shadow mode**: it suggests, your people still do the work, and once a week you grade its suggestions against your answer key — exactly like a new hire's file review. Hold the **promotion conversation** out loud: "It's agreed with us 94% for eight weeks — do we let it do the easy half alone, with spot-checks?"
- **Where it lives:** the weekly grading takes one hour; it replaces an hour of the QA you already do.
- **What the team sees:** the robot took the part of the job everyone hates (hold music, portal logins) — and *people* decided when to trust it. That sequence, in that order, is your culture strategy.
- **Ready for Stair 4 when:** you've promoted one helper one rung — on evidence, out loud, with the team watching.

### STAIR 4 — "Let it rank, then let it draft" *(year 2)*
- **What you do:** Let the computer **order the worklist** (it ranks; people still work the accounts). Then let it **draft** — appeal letters your specialist edits and signs, never sends alone. Introduce the fire-drill habit: slip a known-bad draft into the review pile monthly. If your reviewer approves eight in a row without a "no," they've stopped reviewing — the drill is how you know the review is real.
- **The fork appears (say it plainly):** this is where the road choice from the leave-behind starts to matter — *tune what you already own* (Epic-forward), *buy one best tool where a clear winner exists* (usually contract underpayments), or *build the pieces that compound* (the edges). You did not need to answer this until now — and stairs 1–3 made you smarter about the answer.
- **Ready for Stair 5 when:** drafts leave the shop only over a human signature, the fire drills get caught, and one function's numbers moved against the Stair-1 baseline.

### STAIR 5 — "It works, you supervise" *(years 2–3+)*
- **What you do:** First class of work goes fully independent-with-spot-checks — chosen by one test: **would a mistake surface by Friday?** Status checks and posting reconcile against the remit within days — safe. Appeal arguments have no answer key — a person signs those forever. The huddle board goes live (the command center is just your huddle, continuous). The pod starts becoming the smaller, more senior team from the keynote — through reqs you stop refilling, not walk-outs.
- **This is the 5K finish line.** What comes after — machine-readable contracts, prevention at the source, the full 2030 org — is the same staircase continued, and by now they're climbing it with evidence instead of faith.

**The staircase close:** *"Notice what you never did: you never bet the program on a moonshot, never bought before standardizing, never trusted before grading. Every stair was a thing you already knew how to do. The marathon is just 5Ks laid end to end."*

## 3. The interactives — touch and feel (three, escalating; ~30 min total inside the block)

### INTERACTIVE 1 — "Grade the Agent" *(the centerpiece · 15–18 min · tables)*
The room *becomes* the human in observation mode for ten minutes — the core mechanic of the entire 2030 model, experienced hands-on, zero jargon.

- **Materials:** per table, a packet of **six one-page denial cases** (realistic: payer, denial code, one-paragraph clinical context, dollar amount) each with an **agent-recommended action and two-sentence draft appeal rationale**; grading slips (AGREE / DISAGREE / why, and seconds-spent). **Case 5 is the plant:** plausible, confident, and subtly wrong (cites the wrong payer's policy, or misses a timely-filing bar that moots the appeal).
- **Run:** 8 minutes of individual grading at pace ("your queue has 43 more behind these — work like it"), then table compare.
- **Debrief beats (each lands one concept in their bones):**
  1. *"Congratulations — you just ran observation mode."* Everything the keynote called governance, you just did with a pencil.
  2. *"What was your table's agreement rate? At what rate, over how many cases, would you let this thing send appeals alone?"* — they will negotiate numbers out loud. *"You just invented the promotion gate. That's the autonomy ladder — you built it in four minutes because you've been promoting humans your whole career."*
  3. **The reveal:** *"Case 5 was wrong. Who caught it? … Now be honest: how fast were you grading by case 5?"* — the attention lesson (the sim's rubber-stamping finding) experienced, not lectured. *"That's why real shops slip a known-bad into the pile monthly. A review that never says no isn't a review."*
  4. Hold up the stack of grading slips: *"Fifty of these graded by your best analyst is the answer key everything else stands on. It's also worth money — and if a vendor is in the loop, your contract decides who keeps it. Ask that question this year, while you're small enough that they'll say yes."*
- **Why this works for THIS room:** it's paper, it's denials, it's their day job. And they leave having personally operated the trust machinery — graded, promoted, caught a decoy — which converts "AI governance" from an abstraction into muscle memory.

### INTERACTIVE 2 — "Two Columns" *(5 min · individual · the Monday move)*
- Hand out a realistic one-page **denial log** printout. Ask: *"What two columns would you add Monday so that, two years from now, a computer could learn from this log?"* Let tables shout; land on **owner attribution** and **appeal outcome**.
- The point, said once and hard: *"This is the only urgent thing you heard today. Everything else can wait a quarter. The tape you don't start recording this year cannot be bought back later — at any price."*
- Everyone writes their own shop's two columns on a take-home card. It's the cheapest commitment device in the whole session — and it's Stair 1.

### INTERACTIVE 3 — "Find Your Stair" *(5 min · live poll + a normalizing reveal)*
- Poll 1: *"Which stair is your shop honestly on?"* (0 – haven't started / 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 / 5). Expect the mass at 0–1. Reveal and say so: *"Look at the room. You are not behind. The room is where you are — and the national data says the same thing."* (Ties to the Readiness Mirror's 7%-very-prepared benchmark.)
- Poll 2 (archetype self-ID, translated to plain bets): *"Which sentence sounds most like your organization? (a) We'll ride what Epic gives us. (b) We buy the best tool for each job. (c) We're handing it to a partner. (d) We want to own the engine."* — sets up the leave-behind roadmaps without a minute of archetype theory on stage. For (c) tables, one protective sentence: *"Fine as a bridge — just make sure your data, your answer keys, and your exit rights are in the contract, or you can never come home."*

## 4. The House Rules — the simulation's insights, translated to floor language

Present as a leave-behind card (and 3–4 on stage, max): *"We ran a four-year implementation simulation of a nine-hospital system and had two thirty-year operators tear the findings apart. Here's what survived — written the way your best floor supervisor would say it."*

| # | The house rule (their language) | What it's translating (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Only let the computer work alone on jobs where you'd catch a mistake by Friday.** If an error could hide for a month, a person stays in the loop — no matter how accurate the demo was. | Verifiability-over-accuracy autonomy promotion |
| 2 | **A reviewer who never says no has stopped reviewing.** Slip a known-bad into the pile monthly, like a fire drill. Score the system, not the person. | HITL decay / decoy seeding |
| 3 | **Your team's graded answer keys are worth money. Read the vendor contract to see who keeps them.** Ask one question: who owns the graded work if the vendor is ever sold? | Graded-judgment IP / change-of-control |
| 4 | **Don't let HR freeze the same people the robot needs as babysitters.** The freeze list and the pilot staffing plan have to be the same conversation. | Freeze-wave × supervision-labor collision |
| 5 | **Keep enough people who remember how to do it by hand — and have them do it by hand one day a quarter.** A backup plan nobody has rehearsed is a rumor, not a plan. | Rollback onto a vanished workforce / live-fire drills |
| 6 | **The tool that finds money you're owed also finds money you owe. Budget the refunds and tell compliance before you turn it on.** Your team is watching what you do with those flags. | 60-day overpayment conveyor / relator risk |
| 7 | **When your denial rate drops, check that downcodes and pends dropped too — or the money just moved rooms.** Put a "pended dollars by payer" report next to the denial dashboard. | Payer taxonomy exit / yield rotation |
| 8 | **Agree with your CFO — in writing, before the first dollar — how savings get counted.** If finance can't find the savings in the budget, the program dies at the next CFO. | GL-invisible savings / cost-center spine |
| 9 | **When the easy work leaves, the hard work is all that's left — and it burns people out.** Leave some easy work in the queues on purpose, and defend it when the cost-cutters find it. | Case-mix floor (85 vs 70) / burnout margin |
| 10 | **If the score decides someone's job, the score has to be fair, explainable, and kept in your own systems.** Post the criteria before the contest, not after. | Gate validation / adverse impact / score custody |

**Delivery guidance:** never present all ten from stage — pick the three that pay off the interactives (Rules 1, 2, 3 land directly out of "Grade the Agent"). The card does the rest.

## 5. Where it fits in the session

**Recommended for an early-journey room (which this is):** make "The First 5K" the spine of the interactive block, and let the Lab's redesign exercise become the *advanced* option:

| Time | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | **Find Your Stair** (polls + the "you are not behind" reveal) |
| 0:05–0:23 | **Grade the Agent** (the centerpiece + debrief beats 1–4) |
| 0:23–0:28 | **Two Columns** (the Monday move) |
| 0:28–0:38 | **The Staircase walkthrough** (stairs 1–5, with the Rosetta Stone lines and 3 house rules) |
| 0:38–0:40 | Closing poll: *"Which stair-1 move do you start Monday?"* (SOP sprint / two columns / answer key / name the owner / garage check) — feeds the Readiness Mirror commitment wall |

- **Keynote adjustment (important):** for this room, the 2030 end state gets *five minutes as direction-setting*, not thirty as destination — then hand off: *"That's the marathon. Nobody runs it Monday. Here's the 5K."* The full POV depth moves to the leave-behind.
- **Leave-behind packet additions:** the styled archetype roadmaps (with the "choosing the road" page rewritten as the four plain bets from Interactive 3), the Rosetta Stone card, the House Rules card, and the Stair-1 checklist.
- **Bridge Builder placement:** offered as the "when you're on stair 3+" tool — mentioned, not demoed, for this room.

## 6. Materials & risks

**Materials:** case packets (6 cases × 1 page, one planted error — build from real-but-scrubbed denial patterns; legal review), grading slips, denial-log printout, two-columns take-home card, Rosetta Stone card, House Rules card, staircase wall graphic or table mat, polls loaded.

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Advanced attendees find it basic | The archetype polls surface them; hand them the Bridge Builder and the full roadmaps at the break — "you're on stair 3; here's your tooling." The Grade-the-Agent debrief beats (promotion gates, decoys, answer-key IP) have teeth even for mature shops. |
| The plant (case 5) embarrasses whoever missed it | Never call on individuals; ask for table counts. Frame as designed: "we built it to slip past you — that's the lesson, not a grade." |
| "Which road should WE pick?" derails the staircase | The deferral is the feature: "stairs 1–3 are identical on every road; you'll pick the road in a year, smarter." Roadmaps in the packet for the eager. |
| Time pressure | Interactive 2 folds into Interactive 1's debrief (2 min); staircase compresses to stairs 1–3 + "4 and 5 are in your packet." |
| It reads as dumbed-down | It isn't — it's *translated*. The content is the full ontology sequencing (Phase 0, waves, gates) wearing floor clothes; the traceability note in the roadmaps doc backs every stair. |
