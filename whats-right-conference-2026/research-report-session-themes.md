# What Rev Cycle SVPs & CFOs Aren't Getting Anywhere Else
## Research Report: Theme Areas for the "What's Right In Healthcare" 2-Hour AI & Automation Block

**Prepared for:** AI & Automation Revenue Cycle Capability Lead, Huron
**Date:** July 2026
**Method:** Multi-angle research sweep across 23 sources (2024–2026), prioritizing primary survey research of the exact target audience — HFMA (Feb 2026, n=95; Pulse/AKASA Apr 2025, n=519 CFOs & rev cycle leaders; C-suite survey late 2025, n=182), Guidehouse/HFMA (2024, n=134; 2026, n=191), Experian Health State of Claims 2025, AHA 2026 Workforce Scan, BCG AI workforce research 2026, Health Affairs (Stanford UR AI analysis, Jan 2026), Black Book 2026 (n=882), plus executive press (Becker's, Fierce Healthcare, Healthcare Dive, Healthcare Brew). All claims carry direct source quotes. Three headline claims were additionally adversarially verified (3-0, 3-0, 2-0 verifier votes); the remainder are extracted verbatim from the cited sources but did not complete the adversarial-verification pass due to a compute limit — noted for transparency.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

## The one-sentence answer

Your audience is saturated on **"why AI" and "what to buy"** — what nobody is giving them is **how to be the executive who leads the transition**: redesigning the operating model, converting the workforce, governing the payer arms race, and building their own personal AI fluency. The data says they know this about themselves: **80% are acting on GenAI, but only ~7% say their teams are "very prepared" for the future revenue cycle.** That 73-point gap between activity and readiness is your session.

## The five load-bearing findings

1. **The buy-in battle is over; the readiness battle hasn't started.** 80% of health systems are exploring/piloting/implementing GenAI in rev cycle (up 38% in under two years — AKASA/HFMA, n=519). Self-reported AI literacy jumped from 28% to 62% in one year (Experian). Yet only ~7% of finance/rev-cycle leaders call their teams "very prepared" for the future revenue cycle (HFMA Feb 2026 — **adversarially verified 3-0**), and HFMA's C-suite survey found executives self-rate as *unprepared or only somewhat prepared* to deploy AI. Any minute spent convincing them of AI's value is a wasted minute — and they will recognize it instantly as vendor noise.

2. **The dominant failure mode is "pilot purgatory," and the causes are organizational, not technical.** Only 27% deploy AI at scale; 53% sit in pilots (**verified 3-0**). 67% believe AI can improve claims, but only 14% use it on denials (Experian). The named stall points across every survey: cost/ROI ambiguity (the #1 barrier at 52.5% of large systems), fragmented integration (58% say automation is fragmented across tools/departments/vendors — Black Book), trust (41% find it hard to fully trust AI results), and change-management burden. MIT's much-cited finding — >95% of enterprise GenAI investment shows no measurable ROI — is landing in their board decks right now. Nobody in their vendor ecosystem has an incentive to talk honestly about why pilots die; you do.

3. **The underserved strategic content is operating-model redesign, not technology.** HFMA's own framing (**verified 2-0**): *"The future revenue cycle cannot be automated one task at a time; it must be redesigned end to end… not asking 'How do we do this faster?' but 'Why does this step exist at all?'"* AHA: AI only delivers when paired with redesigned processes. BCG's 10-20-70 rule: 70% of AI value comes from people/process change, and "future-built" companies are 5x more likely to do strategic workforce planning. This is exactly your Rev Cycle 2030 keynote thesis — the research says lean *harder* into the operating-model half and lighter on the technology half.

4. **The payer AI arms race is the most emotionally live, least well-served topic.** It is now explicit and on-the-record: Centene's CEO said on an earnings call they are deploying payment-integrity AI *specifically because* hospitals are adopting rev-cycle AI; Elevance's CEO accused hospitals of using AI to inflate coding acuity. One nonprofit system saw denials/underpayments rise >50% in two years as payers adopted AI claims review. In the 2026 Guidehouse survey, 88% of provider executives rank payer challenges among their top three stressors — payer friction now outranks technology, staffing, and cost-to-collect. Health Affairs (Stanford, based on evaluation of 21 deployed UR AI tools) concludes governance on *both* sides has failed to keep pace. Vendors sell weapons for this war; nobody is helping executives think about *strategy, escalation dynamics, and where this ends* (agent-vs-agent adjudication? data-sharing détente? regulatory intervention?).

5. **Executive personal AI fluency is a proven organizational differentiator — which elevates your third segment from "nice tips" to strategic imperative.** BCG: at companies capturing the most AI value, 88% of managers visibly role-model AI in decisions and daily work, vs. 25% at laggards. Senior executives are *structurally* the slowest to build fluency (their days are meetings and approvals, not automatable tasks — Becker's). 77% of CEOs call AI transformative, but only 44% trust their CIO to navigate it — meaning the executive can't delegate this. HFMA's C-suite research ranks the CFO's needed future skills as: innovation/transformation, payer relations, digital technology, AI competency, risk assessment. Frame Segment 3 not as productivity hacks but as: *your personal AI practice is the single highest-leverage culture intervention you own.*

## What's saturated vs. what's scarce (the content map)

| SATURATED — do not do | SCARCE — where the session wins |
|---|---|
| Why AI matters / market sizing | Why 53% of pilots never scale — honest failure autopsy |
| Use-case catalogs and demos of vendor product | The 2030 operating model: org chart, roles, governance, sequencing |
| ROI promises and vendor case studies | ROI discipline: how leading CIOs/CFOs actually gate AI spend ("no funding on hope") |

# DETAILED REPORT BY THEME

## Theme 1: The Readiness Gap — bought in, not prepared

**Evidence.** HFMA's February 2026 survey (n=95): 44% "somewhat prepared," barely 7% "very prepared" for the future revenue cycle *(adversarially verified 3-0)*. HFMA's late-2025 C-suite survey (n=182, incl. 125 CFOs): executives on average rate themselves "not prepared or only somewhat prepared to deploy AI." Meanwhile adoption signals are maxed: 80% acting on GenAI (AKASA/HFMA, n=519, Apr 2025); 78% already using automation/AI on manual processes by 2024 (Guidehouse); AI-literacy confidence 28%→62% in one year (Experian 2025). Only 39% of CFOs expect their AI/tech investments to actually reduce costs (HFMA C-suite survey) — belief in the technology coexists with skepticism about *their own organization's ability to capture the value*.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Vendors profit from adoption, not readiness. Peer networks trade adoption stories (what we bought), rarely capability confessions (what we couldn't absorb). Conferences program success narratives.

**Implication for the session.** The opening move that instantly differentiates you from every vendor: *assume full buy-in, name the readiness gap out loud, and make the whole session about closing it.* The 7%-vs-80% contrast is a strong cold-open statistic — and polling the room against it ("how prepared is YOUR team?") converts it into engagement.

## Theme 2: Pilot Purgatory — why AI initiatives stall, and what separates scalers

**Evidence — the stall.** 27% at scale vs. 53% in pilots (HFMA, *verified 3-0*). 40% actually piloting/implementing GenAI vs. 39% only "exploring" (AKASA/HFMA). 67% believe AI improves claims / 14% use it on denials (Experian); only ~20% apply AI to denials at all (Bain 2025) — despite denials being the top pain point. Adoption bifurcated by capital: 64% of large systems piloting/implementing vs. 20% of $500M–$1B systems.

**Evidence — the named causes.** Cost/budget the single biggest challenge (52.5% of large systems); integration with fragmented legacy EHR/billing environments; trust (41% "difficult to fully trust AI's results"); HIPAA; training burden; payer-specific rule handling (Experian). 73% have automation in ≥1 workflow but 58% describe it as fragmented across tools, departments, or suppliers (Black Book, n=882) — the stall is *orchestration*, not appetite. MIT: >95% of $30–40B enterprise GenAI investment shows no measurable ROI. Becker's failure-mechanism reporting: black-box models trained only on historical claims data replicate embedded errors and over-flag records, creating a "hidden factory" of manual rework that eats the ROI.

**Evidence — what scalers do differently (named health system leaders, on record).**
- **Reid Health (CIO Muhammad Siddiqui):** early failure = choosing promising tools before defining the problem; "we learned that the hard way."
- **Mass General Brigham (CTO Nallan Sriraman):** stopped ungoverned pilots, installed an enterprise AI governance framework, starts from operational need and outcome.
- **Baptist Health (CIO Aaron Miri):** the *opposite* regret — "we overindexed on caution instead of accelerating where we had clear use cases"; funding rule: no AI "on hope," every initiative needs defined ROI (clinician time returned or direct financial impact).
- **HFMA's architecture pattern:** leaders are building a unified orchestration layer — an "AI fabric" across EHR, point solutions, and internal models — with explainable, auditable decisions, replacing vendor-by-vendor point-solution accumulation.
- **BCG:** concentrate on 3–4 enterprise priorities, not dozens of use cases.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Every vendor claims their tool escapes pilot purgatory; no vendor autopsy is credible. Peer discussion surfaces anecdotes but not pattern-level synthesis. The two *opposite* failure modes (Reid's too-fast vs. Baptist's too-slow) are exactly the kind of nuance sales decks flatten.

**Implication for the session.** A "pattern room" segment — here are the five stall signatures and the five scaler behaviors, from named organizations and our own client portfolio + Huron's AI Acceleration Council — is high-value, non-pitch content. It also gives attendees a self-diagnostic they can run Monday morning.

## Theme 3: Operating-Model Redesign — the actual 2030 work

**Evidence.** HFMA (*verified 2-0*): end-to-end redesign, not task automation; "Putting automation on top of a broken process doesn't fix the process; it scales the dysfunction"; the right question is "Why does this step exist at all?" AHA 2026 Workforce Scan: AI "works best when paired with redesigned processes," and expanding AI workflows create *new* bottlenecks in training, governance, and change management. BCG: future-built companies are 5x more likely to do strategic workforce planning — reshaping job architectures and org structures *with AI at the core*. Structural signals that redesign is already reaching the org chart: ~60% of executives say a **chief AI innovation officer** will be the most important new C-suite role within three years (30% of CFOs name it #1); **"CFO command centers"** are emerging as an executive layer above RCM operations because 66% say current RCM analytics are insufficient for CFO-grade revenue-predictability decisions (Black Book); Black Book's framing that rev cycle has reached its **"boardroom moment"** — a governance topic, not a back-office one.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Operating-model design is consultant territory — but most consulting conversations are engagement-scoped, not peer-comparative. Vendors are structurally biased toward inserting their product into the *existing* process. No one is showing executives what the 2030 org chart, spans, roles, and governance forums actually look like.

**Implication for the session.** This is your keynote's natural second act. The research strongly supports making the *interactive* segment operating-model-centric: e.g., have the room stress-test the 2030 org design, or run a "kill this step" exercise on a universally hated workflow (prior auth status checks, credit-balance resolution). It converts the keynote's vision into something they can steal.

## Theme 4: Workforce Transition — the change already underway and the leadership it demands

**Evidence.** The transition is empirical, not speculative: ambient listening and autonomous coding are already displacing traditional coding/documentation work; coders and documentation specialists are moving into exception management and complex-case review; leaders' stated plan is redeployment into upstream problem-solving, exception-based strategy, patient experience, and *oversight of AI decision-making* (HFMA). AHA: systems are upskilling and creating net-new digital-fluency roles rather than pure headcount cuts, plus cross-training, remote/hybrid, and mobile workforce pools. BCG's 10-20-70: 70% of AI value is people/process; future-built companies plan to upskill >50% of employees (laggards ~20%) and are ~4x more likely to run structured AI-learning programs *with protected learning time*. Economic backdrop: labor ≈60% of hospital expense, workforce costs +5.6% in 2025 (AHA); 43% of providers understaffed (Experian); ~80% already outsource some rev cycle and rank outsourcing *above* automation as their top staffing strategy (Guidehouse 2024). Retention now hinges on flexibility, growth, meaningful work (AHA) — meaning the AI story you tell your staff *is* your retention strategy.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Vendors avoid the workforce topic (it's the awkward part of their ROI math). HR conferences discuss reskilling generically, without rev-cycle role specificity. Nobody gives executives the practical playbook: which roles convert to what, on what timeline, with what skills bridge, and how to communicate it without triggering attrition of your best people.

**Implication for the session.** A concrete "role evolution map" (biller → exception strategist; coder → AI auditor/clinical-context reviewer; front-end rep → patient financial advocate) with the reskilling math and the retention-risk framing is content they can hand their HR partner on Monday. Also a natural poll: "What % of your team could you honestly redeploy today?"

## Theme 5: The Payer AI Arms Race — strategy, not tooling

**Evidence — it's explicit now.** Centene CEO Sarah London (Jul 2025 earnings call): "As [hospitals] integrate AI into the revenue cycle, we're integrating AI into payment integrity to make sure that we are… keeping pace." Elevance CEO Gail Boudreaux (same season): "isolated pockets" of hospitals using AI to "increase documented acuity." One nonprofit system: denials/underpayments +>50% over two years as payers adopted AI claims review. Former payer chief data officer Fawad Butt: the future is "agent wars galore."

**Evidence — it's the top executive stressor.** Guidehouse/HFMA 2026 (n=191): 88% rank payer challenges top-three; prior auth delays and workforce round out the top stressors — while cost-to-collect *fell* as a stressor (25%→12%), plausibly because automation is already relieving it. Guidehouse 2024: 88% claims disagreements blocking payment; 74% rising prior-auth delays. Experian 2025: 41% of providers now face denial rates >10% (up from 30% in 2022); 68% say clean claims are *harder* than a year ago. Adonis 2026: 62% cite denials/underpayments as top revenue obstacle (now outranking staffing); 48% cite payers' opaque, frequently changing adjudication rules as the #1 collections drag; 47% lost 3–4% of net patient revenue to denials/underpayments/timely-filing; 36% say denials are now an executive-level agenda item; teams spend 51–75 hours/week on denial work. What leaders want from AI is payer-facing: 66% rate automated denial follow-up "very important"; 29% say payer transparency is AI's single most impactful 2026 outcome.

**Evidence — the governance vacuum and asymmetries.** Health Affairs (Stanford; evaluation of 21 deployed predictive/generative UR tools): governance on both sides "has not fully met the challenge"; documented risk categories = weak human-in-the-loop, poor user understanding, opaque determinations, task underperformance, automation bias, social consequences. Third front: patient-side appeal automation (e.g., Claimable) will raise appeal volumes regardless of what providers do. Asymmetry: large systems can resource AI appeals; rural/independent hospitals can't — the arms race compounds advantage.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Vendors sell ammunition, not strategy. Peer forums trade denial-rate war stories, not game theory. Nobody is walking executives through escalation scenarios (agent-vs-agent adjudication, payer data-sharing détentes, gold-carding expansion, state/federal regulatory intervention) or the counterintuitive question: *where does cooperating beat escalating?*

**Implication for the session.** This is the highest-energy candidate for the interactive segment: it's the topic they're angriest about, it's genuinely strategic (CFO-level, not director-level), and a scenario exercise ("it's 2028, payer agents auto-adjudicate 90% of claims — what did you do in 2026 to be ready?") produces real debate. It also connects directly to the Rev Cycle 2030 keynote: the 2030 human roles you describe are payer *relationship* roles.

## Theme 6: Governance, Trust & Auditability — the new license to operate

**Evidence.** 63% of provider respondents say AI auditability/explainability are *mandatory* (Black Book); rev cycle at its "boardroom moment." Effective governance structure per Becker's synthesis: committees combining clinical informatics, data science, and frontline users; projects gated by problem statement, success metrics, integration plan, change management. MGB's on-record move: halt ungoverned pilots, install enterprise framework. Health Affairs provides a ready-made six-item risk checklist (above). Root technical trust issue: black-box models trained on claims-only data can't see clinical context and inherit historical errors — executives should interrogate training provenance ("how was it trained, by whom, does it encode clinical logic?"). Boards are reading the MIT 95% statistic; CFO ROI skepticism (only 39% expect cost reduction) makes governance the credibility bridge.

**Why other channels don't serve it.** Vendor governance talk is compliance theater. Legal/compliance conferences treat AI risk generically. Nobody hands the rev-cycle executive a *practical* governance kit calibrated to revenue cycle: what to gate, what to audit, what to ask vendors, what the board will ask *you*.

**Implication for the session.** Governance is better woven through Themes 2/3/5 than given its own block (it's important but low-energy as a standalone). A one-page "board-ready AI governance checklist" as a leave-behind carries this theme without spending stage time.

## Theme 7: Executive Personal AI Fluency — the leader as the culture intervention

**Evidence.** BCG: 88% of managers at future-built companies role-model AI in decision-making and daily operations vs. 25% at laggards — visible leader usage is among the strongest observed correlates of AI value capture. Becker's: senior executives are structurally slowest to build fluency (meeting/approval-dominated days offer no natural practice reps); some CEOs now mandate habit-level fixes (use GenAI before any search engine); 77% of CEOs call AI transformative while only 44% believe their CIO is equipped — so fluency can't be delegated. Henry Ford Health CEO Bob Riney's framing: paralysis vs. disciplined lean-in. HFMA C-suite research: CFO future-skill stack = innovation/transformation, payer relations, digital technology, AI competency, risk assessment; leaders must personally "separate hype from reality" and build governance — and the emerging C-suite identity is "interconnected, innovative, data-driven and AI savvy."

**Why other channels don't serve it.** This is the topic executives literally cannot get elsewhere: vendors don't care about the executive's own practice; peer forums are too performative for leaders to admit they don't use these tools; generic "AI for productivity" content isn't calibrated to a healthcare CFO's actual week (board prep, payer negotiation, difficult conversations, strategy memos, talent decisions).

**Implication for the session.** Your Segment 3 instinct is validated — but reframe it. Not "tips and tricks": the through-line is *the BCG 88%-vs-25% finding — your visible practice is the culture strategy*. Showing your real, imperfect, personal use (leadership coaching, negotiation prep, strategy red-teaming, board-question anticipation, feedback drafting) models the exact behavior the data says distinguishes winning organizations. Live demonstration with your actual workflows (not polished vendor demos) is the anti-pitch.

## Theme 8 (thin evidence — flagged honestly): Executive session engagement formats

The research sweep returned little rigorous evidence on executive-learning formats (the one academic source didn't survive quality screening). What the data *does* support: this audience's own survey answers create a natural peer-benchmarking mechanic — nearly every theme above has a published statistic the room can poll itself against in real time ("the national number is X — what's yours?"). Anonymous live polling against national benchmarks is low-risk, defensible, and generates the "where do I stand vs. my peers" value executives consistently seek from closed-door sessions. Treat other format claims as craft judgment, not research findings.
