# Build-Out #1 — "The Arms Race War Game: Revenue Cycle 2028"
## Full session design for the interactive block (30–45 min)

**Slot:** Segment 2 of the 2-hour block, immediately following the Revenue Cycle 2030 keynote
**Duration:** 40 min core design, with a 30-min compression and 45-min extension noted throughout
**Audience:** ~5–8 tables of 6–8 Revenue Cycle SVPs and CFOs
**Theme anchors:** Payer AI arms race (Theme 5), operating-model redesign (Theme 3)

---

## 1. Why this exercise (the design logic)

- It targets the topic the research shows is **most emotionally live and least well-served**: 88% of provider executives rank payer challenges among their top three stressors (Guidehouse/HFMA 2026), the arms race is now explicit on payer earnings calls, and vendors sell weapons for this war while nobody teaches strategy.
- It is **CFO-native**: game-theoretic, capital-allocation, board-level. It cannot be mistaken for a product demo.
- It **completes the keynote**: Rev Cycle 2030 says the future human roles are payer/patient/clinician *relationship* roles. The war game is where the room discovers *why* — because in an agent-vs-agent world, the durable human work is negotiating the rules of engagement.
- The "act differently Monday" mechanism is built in: every table ends by naming its **2026 move** — the thing you'd need to start now to be ready for the 2028 scenario.

**Session promise (say it out loud at the start):** *"For the next 40 minutes, no one is selling you anything. We're going to play out where the payer-provider AI escalation actually goes, and work backwards to what you should do this year."*

---

## 2. Run of show (40-minute version)

| Time | Beat | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | **Cold open: "This is not hypothetical"** | 3 evidence slides (see §3). No editorializing — let the quotes land. |
| 0:04–0:07 | **Scenario brief + rules** | Read the 2028 scenario aloud (§4). Assign table archetypes (§5). Explain the strategy round (§6). |
| 0:07–0:19 | **Strategy round (12 min)** | Tables allocate 100 strategy points across four postures and select three concrete 2026–2027 moves from the move menu. One person records on the table canvas. |
| 0:19–0:26 | **Inject round (7 min)** | Each table draws one inject card (§7) — a curveball that stress-tests their posture. Table must decide: hold strategy or adapt, and what they'd trade. |
| 0:26–0:36 | **Report-outs + facilitated debrief (10 min)** | 3–4 tables report (90 sec each): posture split, one move, how the inject changed their thinking. Facilitator runs the debrief provocations (§8). |
| 0:36–0:40 | **Synthesis: "Where this ends" + the 2026 move** | Facilitator closes with the three endgames (§9), the tie back to the 2030 keynote, and a live poll: "What's YOUR 2026 move?" |

**30-min compression:** cut inject round to a single facilitator-announced inject for all tables (2 min), take 2 report-outs, tighten strategy round to 10 min.
**45-min extension:** all tables report out; add the second poll ("Which posture did your table underweight, and why?"); extend debrief with the détente provocation (§8, Q4).

## 3. Cold-open evidence slides (3 slides, verbatim quotes only)

**Slide 1 — The other side has already declared.**
> "As [hospitals] integrate AI into the revenue cycle, we're integrating AI into payment integrity to make sure that we are… keeping pace with all of that."
> — Sarah London, CEO, Centene (July 2025 earnings call)

> "Isolated pockets" of hospitals are using AI to "increase documented acuity."
> — Gail Boudreaux, CEO, Elevance (July 2025 earnings call)

**Slide 2 — The escalation is measurable.**
- One nonprofit health system: underpayments/initial denials **+50%+ over two years** as payers adopted AI claims review (Healthcare Brew, Oct 2025)
- 48% of RCM leaders: payers' **frequent, opaque adjudication-rule changes** were the #1 factor hurting 2025 collections (Adonis 2026)
- 47% lost **3–4% of net patient revenue** to denials, underpayments, or timely-filing (Adonis 2026)

**Slide 3 — And nobody is governing it.**
> "To date, institutional governance by insurers and providers has not fully met the challenge of ensuring responsible use."
> — Health Affairs, Jan 2026 (Stanford analysis of 21 deployed utilization-review AI tools)

> The future is "agent wars galore."
> — Fawad Butt, former payer chief data officer

**Facilitator line to transition:** *"Every vendor in your inbox wants to sell you a weapon for this war. Almost nobody is asking the CFO question: what's the strategy, where does escalation end, and what's the move you make in 2026 so you're not cornered in 2028? So let's play it forward."*

## 4. The scenario (read aloud + printed on table canvas)

> **It is Q3 2028.**
> Your major commercial payers now auto-adjudicate the large majority of claims with AI agents. Denial and underpayment decisions are generated in seconds, at volumes your appeal teams cannot match manually. Payers' models retrain weekly; adjudication behavior shifts without notice.
>
> Your organization runs its own AI: denial prediction, auto-drafted appeals, documentation-integrity agents. So does every large system in your market. Meanwhile, patient-side AI services file appeals directly on your patients' behalf — some duplicate or conflict with yours, and their letters cite your prices.
>
> Two state legislatures are debating AI-transparency requirements for utilization review. CMS has signaled interest. Nothing has passed.
>
> Your board asks a simple question at the Q3 meeting: **"Are we winning this, and what is it costing us?"** Your CFO's honest answer is: administrative cost per claim is *up*, days-in-AR is flat, and both sides' AI spend is compounding annually.
>
> **Your task:** As the executive team, set your strategic posture for 2029–2030 — and identify what you wish you had started in 2026.

## 5. Table archetypes (assign one per table; laminated card)

Assigning fictional archetypes de-risks confidentiality, levels the room, and surfaces the asymmetry insight from the research (large systems can resource the arms race; smaller ones cannot).

| Archetype | Profile | Built-in tension |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Meridian Health** | $9B multi-state system with an academic medical center; substantial AI budget; 60% commercial book with two dominant payers | Can afford to escalate — but is the biggest target for acuity-inflation accusations |
| **B. Lakeview Health** | $1.6B regional system; mid-cycle AI pilots stalled; one payer is 45% of commercial revenue | Too big to ignore the war, too small to win an arms race with its dominant payer |
| **C. Prairie Community Health** | $380M rural/community system; no data-science staff; rising prior-auth burden; margins near zero | Cannot arm up at all — must find partnership, consortium, or advocacy plays |
| **D. Summit Medical Partners** | Large physician enterprise / medical group; high prior-auth exposure; owns no hospital leverage | Feels payer AI first and hardest; considering payer-delegated arrangements |

(With 5–8 tables, duplicate A and B — the contrast between two tables playing the same archetype differently is itself debrief material.)

## 6. The strategy round: four postures + move menu

### The four postures (tables allocate 100 points across them)

| Posture | Thesis | The bet | The risk (say these out loud when introducing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. ARM UP** (Escalate) | Match and exceed payer AI: denial prediction, auto-appeals at scale, payer-behavior analytics, acuity-capture AI | Volume war is winnable with better models | Retaliation; "supercharging flaws" on both sides (Health Affairs); admin cost spiral; audit/PR exposure on acuity capture |
| **2. NEGOTIATE** (Détente) | Trade transparency for friction reduction: gold-carding, touchless-claim agreements, shared data standards, joint AI-governance terms in contracts | Both sides are losing money on the war; there's a deal to be made | Unilateral disarmament if the payer defects; requires leverage you may not have |
| **3. CHANGE THE GAME** (Restructure) | Make adjudication matter less: eliminate denial-generating steps at the source (documentation at point of care, front-end financial clearance), shift book toward value-based/capitated arrangements | The best appeal is the claim that can't be denied; the best claim is one that no longer exists as a fight | Slow and capital-heavy; doesn't relieve 2026–2028 pressure |
| **4. CHANGE THE RULES** (Advocate) | Regulatory and coalition play: state AI-transparency laws for UR, prior-auth reform, AHA/state-association coalitions, strategic use of the patient-side appeal wave and media | The referee can end the war faster than either army | Slow, uncertain, and disclosure rules will bind *your* AI too |

### The move menu (tables pick THREE concrete 2026–2027 moves)

**Arm Up moves**
- A1. Stand up a payer-behavior intelligence function (track adjudication-rule drift weekly, by payer)
- A2. Deploy auto-appeal agents on your top 3 denial categories with human-in-the-loop QA
- A3. Build/buy denial-prediction integrated at claim creation, not post-denial

**Negotiate moves**
- N1. Put AI-governance terms into your next payer contract cycle: mutual disclosure of AI use in UR/appeals, human-review guarantees, adjudication-rule change notice periods
- N2. Propose a touchless-claims / gold-carding pilot with your #1 payer (auto-approval for high-clean-rate service lines in exchange for documentation transparency)
- N3. Join or form a regional payer-provider data-standards consortium

**Change the Game moves**
- G1. Fund documentation integrity at the point of care (ambient + CDI agents) to cut denial-generating defects at source
- G2. Kill-list project: identify your top 5 denial-generating process steps and eliminate (not automate) at least 2
- G3. Shift 10%+ of commercial book toward arrangements where claim-level adjudication is reduced (capitation, bundles, delegated arrangements)

**Change the Rules moves**
- R1. Sponsor/testify for state UR-AI transparency legislation through your hospital association
- R2. Build the public narrative: publish your denial/underpayment data trends; align with patient-advocacy appeal wave rather than fighting it
- R3. Prepare for disclosure-symmetric regulation: internal audit of *your own* AI's explainability so rules help you rather than hurt you

**Table canvas (11×17 print, one per table):** posture allocation boxes (must total 100), three move slots, one line for "the move we argued about most," one line for "our 2026 move" (filled at the end).

## 7. Inject cards (each table draws ONE at 0:19)

Each card is a factual-pattern curveball. Table has 7 minutes to decide: hold or adapt, and what they'd trade.

**INJECT 1 — The Détente Offer.** Your largest commercial payer offers a "touchless claims" deal: 95% auto-approval on gold-carded service lines, in exchange for **API access to your clinical documentation** and a cap on your appeal volume. Legal flags the data exposure; your CMO loves the prior-auth relief. Accept, counter, or decline — and what's your counter?

**INJECT 2 — Your Vendor Changes Sides.** Your rev-cycle AI vendor — which holds three years of your denial patterns, appeal outcomes, and documentation data — is acquired by a payer-owned services subsidiary. (The room will recognize the Optum/Change Healthcare pattern.) What do you do in the next 90 days, and what should your contract have said in 2026?

**INJECT 3 — The Referee Steps In.** CMS proposes rules: human review required for AI-generated denials, AND mandatory disclosure of AI use in claims submission and appeals — **binding both sides**. Your auto-appeal volume and your acuity-capture AI are now disclosable. Does regulation help or hurt your posture? What do you comment on the proposed rule?

**INJECT 4 — The Third Army.** A patient-facing AI service goes viral in your market, filing appeals on behalf of your patients directly. Appeal volume triples; some of its filings conflict with yours; its letters quote your chargemaster prices; a local reporter calls. Fight it, ignore it, or ally with it?

**INJECT 5 — The Accusation.** Your #2 payer publicly names your system (à la Elevance) for "AI-driven acuity inflation" and demands a coding audit. Your coding-AI vendor cannot fully explain the model's behavior on the flagged cases. Board wants a response by Friday. What's your first move — and what governance would have prevented this?

**Facilitator note:** match injects to archetypes where possible — Inject 1/5 to A tables, 2 to B, 4 to C or D, 3 to any.

## 8. Debrief provocations (facilitator script)

Run after report-outs. Pick 3 of 5 based on what the room produced.

1. **The allocation tell:** *"Most tables put the biggest number on Arm Up. That's also what every vendor is counting on. Notice what that does to the industry P&L: both sides' AI spend compounds and — per the 2028 scenario, which is just the current data extended — cost per claim goes UP. Who actually wins an arms race? The arms dealers. What would it take for this room to weight Negotiate higher?"*
2. **The asymmetry:** *"Listen to what Prairie Community's table just said — they can't play at all. If the arms race compounds advantage, what obligation or opportunity do the large systems in this room have — consortium models, shared utilities, hosting smaller systems on your stack?"*
3. **The 2030 connection (bridge to keynote):** *"In the keynote I argued 2030's smaller rev cycle team is focused on payer, patient, and clinician relationships. This exercise is why. When agents handle the transaction volume, the human work left is exactly what you just did at your tables: setting the rules of engagement, negotiating the détentes, deciding what to escalate. Your future payer-strategy leader looks less like a denials manager and more like a diplomat with a data team."*
4. **The détente test:** *"Inject 1 offered real prior-auth relief for real data exposure. Almost every table hesitated. Here's the uncomfortable question: is that prudence, or is it the same distrust that keeps the war going? What verification terms would make you say yes?"*
5. **The regret question (sets up the close):** *"Every inject had a version of 'what should you have done in 2026?' — the contract clause, the governance, the audit trail, the relationship. That's the point of playing 2028: the moves are available NOW and cheap NOW."*

## 9. Synthesis close: "Where this ends" (2 slides + 1 poll)

**Slide: Three endgames** — every arms race in history resolves one of three ways:
1. **Mutual exhaustion** → someone proposes arms control (payer-provider AI compacts, transparency treaties, touchless-claim standards)
2. **Regulatory intervention** → the referee sets rules (and disclosure will bind both sides — be ready to pass the audit you demand of payers)
3. **Structural bypass** → the fight becomes irrelevant (denial-proof claims at source; payment models without claim-level adjudication)

*"Your portfolio should assume all three. Arm Up buys you time and leverage; it is not an endgame."*

**Closing poll (phones up):** *"What is YOUR 2026 move?"*
- ☐ Payer-behavior intelligence function
- ☐ AI-governance terms in next payer contract
- ☐ Touchless/gold-carding pilot proposal
- ☐ Kill-list: eliminate denial-generating steps
- ☐ Audit my own AI's explainability before someone else does
- ☐ Coalition/advocacy play

Show the room's distribution. Final line: *"Look at this chart. This is what this room — controlling [X]B in NPR — plans to do this year. Remember it when a payer tells you providers aren't organized."*

## 11. Risks & contingencies

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| "This is speculative" pushback | It isn't — return to Slide 1–2: on-record CEO quotes, +50% measured escalation. The only speculation is the *date*. |
| Tables default 100 points to Arm Up | Expected — it's debrief fuel (provocation #1), not a failure. |
| Confidentiality hesitance | Archetypes solve it; remind the room they're playing fictional systems. |
| One voice dominates a table | Canvas requires the recorder to capture "the move we argued about most" — call on that line in report-outs. |
| Timing overrun in strategy round | Visible timer + 2-minute warning; the inject round can compress to a single all-tables inject. |
| Room is smaller than expected (<3 tables) | Convert to single-group fishbowl: facilitator plays the payer, room plays one archetype, injects run sequentially. |
| Energy is low post-keynote | The cold-open quotes are the defibrillator — read the Centene quote slowly and ask, "Who here believes their payers are NOT doing this?" |
