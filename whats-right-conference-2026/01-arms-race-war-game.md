# Build-Out #1 — "The Arms Race War Game: Revenue Cycle 2028"
## Full session design for the interactive block (30–45 min)

**Slot:** Segment 2 of the 2-hour block, immediately following the Revenue Cycle 2030 keynote
**Duration:** 40 min core design, with a 30-min compression and 45-min extension noted throughout
**Audience:** ~5–8 tables of 6–8 Revenue Cycle SVPs and CFOs
**Theme anchors:** Payer AI arms race (Theme 5), operating-model redesign (Theme 3)
**Evidence base:** grounded in the two-study wargame series — `06-ai-wargame-report.md` (Study 1, 8 games) and `07-revised-wargame-report.md` (Wargame 2.0, 14 doctrine-ablated games). This build-out was **revised after Wargame 2.0** — the changes are structural, not cosmetic, and are summarized in §0.

---

## 0. What changed after Wargame 2.0 (read this first if you ran the old version)

The original exercise was built on a two-sided frame: providers vs. payers, "who wins the arms race." Wargame 2.0 ran that question 14 times across three adjudication doctrines and three regulatory worlds and found the frame itself is the trap. The four findings that reshape this exercise:

1. **The arms race has no winner — and escalation was the worst strategy tested.** Across 22 games in two studies, symmetric escalation on the payer's strong surfaces never produced a durable advantage; the only ledger-positive piece of "arming up" was working the *never-appealed ~40% tail*. So "Arm Up" stays on the board — as the seductive trap the debrief exposes, not the default.
2. **The decisive actor is the self-funded employer, and it wasn't even in the old game.** In 14 of 14 games — including the ones with the most activist regulator — the first thing that ever constrained payer behavior was purchaser/contract paper, never a statute or CMS. The employer is now a posture (§6) and the mid-game reveal (§7).
3. **The denial doesn't get defeated — it changes clothes.** Constrain clinical denials and the recovery value rotates within one "turn" to member-invisible surfaces (pre-pay edits, downcodes, COB, subrogation, eligibility, pricing). The scenario (§4) and a new inject now carry this.
4. **Numeric denial-rate corridors are a dead ask** (0 signed in 22 games). What binds is *process paper* — edit-library disclosure, change-notice floors, gold-card criteria, audit rights, fee clawbacks. The move menu is rebuilt accordingly.

**The pedagogical arc is now:** the room walks in believing it's an arms race someone wins; it walks out knowing it's a rotating armistice metered by employers — and that the highest-leverage move is one most of them under-weight on the canvas.

---

## 1. Why this exercise (the design logic)

- It targets the topic the research shows is **most emotionally live and least well-served**: 88% of provider executives rank payer challenges among their top three stressors (Guidehouse/HFMA 2026), the arms race is now explicit on payer earnings calls, and vendors sell weapons for this war while nobody teaches strategy.
- It is **CFO-native**: game-theoretic, capital-allocation, board-level. It cannot be mistaken for a product demo.
- It **completes the keynote**: Rev Cycle 2030 says the future human roles are payer/patient/clinician *relationship* roles. The war game is where the room discovers *why* — and Wargame 2.0 sharpens it: the relationship that actually moves the payer is the one with the **employer** buying the coverage.
- The "act differently Monday" mechanism is built in: every table ends by naming its **2026 move** — and the closing poll options are the exact no-regret moves the 14-game study validated across every doctrine and world.

**Session promise (say it out loud at the start):** *"For the next 40 minutes, no one is selling you anything. We're going to play out where the payer-provider AI escalation actually goes — and I'll tell you up front, we've already run this as a structured wargame 22 times with AI teams on both sides. You're going to reach some of the same conclusions the machines did, and one that surprised us."*

## 2. Run of show (40-minute version)

| Time | Beat | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | **Cold open: "This is not hypothetical"** | 3 evidence slides (see §3). No editorializing — let the quotes land. |
| 0:04–0:07 | **Scenario brief + rules** | Read the 2028 scenario aloud (§4). Assign table archetypes (§5). Explain the strategy round (§6). |
| 0:07–0:19 | **Strategy round (12 min)** | Tables allocate 100 strategy points across **five** postures and select three concrete 2026–2027 moves from the move menu. One person records on the table canvas. |
| 0:19–0:26 | **Inject round (7 min)** | Each table draws one inject card (§7) — a curveball that stress-tests their posture. Table must decide: hold strategy or adapt, and what they'd trade. The **employer-coalition inject** is dealt to at least half the tables. |
| 0:26–0:36 | **Report-outs + facilitated debrief (10 min)** | 3–4 tables report (90 sec each): posture split, one move, how the inject changed their thinking. Facilitator runs the debrief provocations (§8) — including the employer reveal. |
| 0:36–0:40 | **Synthesis: "Where this actually ends" + the 2026 move** | Facilitator closes with the four emergent futures (§9), the tie back to the 2030 keynote, and a live poll: "What's YOUR 2026 move?" |

**30-min compression:** cut inject round to a single facilitator-announced inject for all tables (2 min) — use the **employer-coalition inject** so the reveal still lands; take 2 report-outs, tighten strategy round to 10 min.
**45-min extension:** all tables report out; add the second poll ("Which posture did your table underweight, and why?" — the answer is almost always Ally with the Purchaser); extend debrief with the rotation provocation (§8, Q4).

## 3. Cold-open evidence slides (3 slides, verbatim quotes only)

**Slide 1 — The other side has already declared.**
> "As [hospitals] integrate AI into the revenue cycle, we're integrating AI into payment integrity to make sure that we are… keeping pace with all of that."
> — Sarah London, CEO, Centene (July 2025 earnings call)

> "Isolated pockets" of hospitals are using AI to "increase documented acuity."
> — Gail Boudreaux, CEO, Elevance (July 2025 earnings call)

**Slide 2 — The escalation is measurable, and the money moves.**
- One nonprofit health system: underpayments/initial denials **+50%+ over two years** as payers adopted AI claims review (Healthcare Brew, Oct 2025)
- 48% of RCM leaders: payers' **frequent, opaque adjudication-rule changes** were the #1 factor hurting 2025 collections (Adonis 2026)
- 47% lost **3–4% of net patient revenue** to denials, underpayments, or timely-filing (Adonis 2026)
- **The pattern under it (our 14-game finding): constrain one denial channel and the recovery value rotates within ~a year to the next, less-watched surface — pre-pay edits → downcodes → COB / subrogation / eligibility / pricing. The denial doesn't die. It changes clothes.**

**Slide 3 — And the referee never showed up.**
> "To date, institutional governance by insurers and providers has not fully met the challenge of ensuring responsible use."
> — Health Affairs, Jan 2026 (Stanford analysis of 21 deployed utilization-review AI tools)

- **In 14 of 14 wargames — including the ones with the most aggressive regulator we could model — no statute, court, or CMS action was ever the first thing that constrained payer behavior. Something else was. Hold that thought.**

**Facilitator line to transition:** *"Every vendor in your inbox wants to sell you a weapon for this war. Almost nobody is asking the CFO question: where does this actually go, and what's the move you make in 2026 so you're not cornered in 2028? We've run the answer 22 times. Now it's your table's turn."*

## 4. The scenario (read aloud + printed on table canvas)

> **It is Q3 2028.**
> Your major commercial payers now auto-adjudicate the large majority of claims with AI agents. You fought back: denial prediction, auto-drafted appeals, documentation-integrity agents. So did every large system in your market.
>
> Here's what your dashboard shows. Your **initial clinical-denial rate is actually down** — your appeal engine works. But your net revenue leakage is *flat*, because the payer's recovery engine quietly moved: pre-pay edits, downcoding, DRG validation, and now coordination-of-benefits and pricing recoupments — surfaces your appeal team wasn't built for and your patients never see. Administrative cost per claim is **up** on both sides. The vendor selling you appeals AI also sold the payer its payment-integrity engine.
>
> Meanwhile, a coalition of the **large self-funded employers** in your market — the ones whose workers you treat — has started asking hard questions about what they're paying their carrier's payment-integrity arm, and whether those recoveries are coming out of *their* claims dollars.
>
> Two state legislatures passed AI-transparency laws. Neither has bitten; one isn't operational until 2030.
>
> Your board asks the Q3 question: **"Are we winning this, and what is it costing us?"** Your honest answer: nobody is winning, the fight just relocated, and the one lever you haven't pulled is sitting in that employer coalition.
>
> **Your task:** As the executive team, set your strategic posture for 2029–2030 — and identify what you wish you had started in 2026.

## 5. Table archetypes (assign one per table; laminated card)

Assigning fictional archetypes de-risks confidentiality, levels the room, and surfaces the size-asymmetry the research documents. These are the same four provider archetypes the 14-game study used as its Blue cell — so every archetype's built-in tension is one the wargame actually stress-tested.

| Archetype | Profile | Built-in tension | What the wargame found for this archetype |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Meridian Health** | $9B multi-state system with an academic medical center; substantial AI budget; 60% commercial book with two dominant payers | Can afford to escalate — but is the biggest target for acuity-inflation accusations | Prevention-at-source ("the terrain payers can't buy") and the employer alliance beat its own arms-race spend |
| **B. Lakeview Health** | $1.6B regional system; mid-cycle AI pilots stalled; one payer is 45% of commercial revenue | Too big to ignore the war, too small to win an arms race with its dominant payer | Its 45%-concentration book is a *weak* negotiating base — it won architecture and lost economics whenever it led with it; its abrasion ledger only cashed at renewal |
| **C. Prairie Community Health** | $380M rural/community system; no data-science staff; rising prior-auth burden; margins near zero | Cannot arm up at all — survives only with a BATNA in hand before the payer offer lands | Survived in every game it had a coalition utility or direct-contract BATNA; captured in the ones it didn't |
| **D. Summit Medical Partners** | Large physician enterprise / medical group; high prior-auth exposure; owns no hospital leverage | Feels payer AI first and hardest; the target of payer gold-card and delegation wedges | Gold-card/delegation wedges beat solidarity every time relief wasn't pre-funded; direct employer contracting was its cleanest exit |

(With 5–8 tables, duplicate A and B — the contrast between two tables playing the same archetype differently is itself debrief material.)

## 6. The strategy round: five postures + move menu

### The five postures (tables allocate 100 points across them)

Introduce all five neutrally. Do **not** signal which the wargame favored — the gap between where the room puts its points and what actually worked is the debrief.

| Posture | Thesis | The bet | What 22 games actually showed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. ESCALATE** (Arm Up) | Match and exceed payer AI: denial prediction, auto-appeals at scale, payer-behavior analytics, acuity-capture AI | A volume war is winnable with better models | **The trap.** Symmetric escalation on the payer's strong surfaces never won a durable advantage in any game; only the *never-appealed ~40% tail* paid. Escalation also feeds the "frivolous appeals" and "acuity inflation" counter-narratives. |
| **2. ALLY WITH THE PURCHASER** (Arm the employer) | Feed self-funded employers claim-line evidence for fiduciary audits; co-draft ASO RFP riders; build direct-contract / wraparound options timed to their award cycles | The one who signs the checks can discipline the payer in ways you never can | **The decisive move — and the one rooms under-weight.** In 14/14 games the employer/purchaser was the *first* constraint that bound payer behavior; a single demonstrated anchor-account defection moved a national payer more than three years of provider AI spend. |
| **3. PREVENT & REMOVE** (Change the game) | Prevention-at-source (clean-claim engineering, documentation origination) + dispute removal (prospective/capitated direct contracts) | The best claim is the one that can't be denied; the best dispute is the one that never exists | **Robust everywhere.** The one provider move the payer cannot retaliate against; drove the largest genuine denial declines and was ledger-positive under every doctrine and world. |
| **4. TRACK THE ROTATION** (Recovery-ledger) | Instrument and pre-position on the surfaces the money moves to: pre-pay edits, downcodes, COB, subrogation, eligibility, pricing — and negotiate *contractual* vector-disclosure | The denial changes clothes; win by being where it goes before it gets there | **Where the war actually lives by 2029.** Detection was won by contract paper, not analytics — telemetry ran a rotation behind the money in every game. All four asymmetric-friction games scheduled a successor fight on this ledger for 2031. |
| **5. CHANGE THE RULES** (Advocate) | State AI-transparency laws, prior-auth reform, AHA/association coalitions, litigation, media | The referee can end the war faster than either army | **Decorative — inside this horizon.** Statutes passed unfunded or pre-operational; CMS ran forbearance; courts settled sealed and cash-only. Litigation paid only as a *pricing lever* converted into process terms at leverage peaks — never as a primary theater. |

### The move menu (tables pick THREE concrete 2026–2027 moves)

**Escalate moves**
- E1. Stand up a payer-behavior intelligence function (track adjudication-rule drift weekly, by payer)
- E2. Industrialize appeals on the **never-appealed ~40% tail** (55–67% overturn base rates, no counterparty consent) — funded as a self-liquidating program
- E3. Build/buy denial-prediction integrated at claim creation — *but refuse* symmetric contests on the payer's strongest surfaces (post-pay audit, technical edits)

**Ally-with-the-Purchaser moves**
- P1. Stand up an **employer-alliance desk**: feed self-funded employers claim-line evidence for fiduciary (CAA) audits; co-draft ASO RFP rider language; time asks to their award cycles
- P2. Build a direct-contract / wraparound offering for the jumbo employers whose workers you treat — a dispute-removal BATNA that also arms the payer negotiation
- P3. Supply the coalition the defection threat: help one anchor account credibly price a carrier switch

**Prevent & Remove moves**
- R1. Fund documentation integrity at the point of care (ambient + CDI agents) to cut denial-generating defects at source — the terrain the payer can't audit its way into
- R2. Kill-list project: identify your top 5 denial-generating process steps and eliminate (not automate) at least 2
- R3. For concentrated-payer books, remove disputes from existence: prospective-payment/capitated direct contracts negotiated from a dual-track BATNA

**Track-the-Rotation moves**
- T1. Build the **total-adverse-action dashboard**: pends, downcodes, recoupments, COB, underpayments, timely-filing on one screen with initial denials, by payer, monthly
- T2. Pre-position recovery-ledger capability (COB, subrogation, eligibility, pricing/itemized-bill audit) *before* the payer's engine arrives there
- T3. Negotiate **contractual vector-disclosure** — quarterly adverse-action mix reporting with notice floors and audit triggers (the form that survives; numeric corridors don't)

**Change-the-Rules moves** (kept honest — the wargame's fragile column)
- C1. Sponsor/testify for state UR-AI transparency legislation — treat any win as a citation engine and compliance moat, not relief
- C2. Use litigation as a **pricing lever**: convert any algorithmic-denial settlement's audit/disclosure terms into your renewal drafts
- C3. Draft **preemption-proof**: anchor every protection in bilateral contract terms, not statutory rights (statutes never bit in-window anyway)

**Table canvas (11×17 print, one per table):** posture allocation boxes (must total 100), three move slots, one line for "the move we argued about most," one line for "the posture we gave the fewest points," and one line for "our 2026 move" (filled at the end). The "fewest points" line is deliberate — it usually reads *Ally with the Purchaser*, and it makes the debrief self-scoring.

## 7. Inject cards (each table draws ONE at 0:19)

Each card is a factual-pattern curveball drawn from the wargame record. Table has 7 minutes to decide: hold or adapt, and what they'd trade. **Deal INJECT 1 (the employer reveal) to at least half the tables** — it carries the session's central discovery.

**INJECT 1 — The Purchaser Organizes (the reveal).** The self-funded employer coalition in your market sends every carrier an RFP demanding fee clawbacks on overturned payment-integrity recoveries, auditor-selection rights, and edit-library disclosure — and quietly asks *you* for the claim-line evidence to back it, plus a direct-contract conversation. Your payer, facing an anchor-account defection, suddenly returns your calls. **Do you feed the coalition? What do you ask for in return? And why wasn't this your opening move?**

**INJECT 2 — The Denial Changes Clothes.** Your denial rate dropped 1.5 points — a board-deck win. But this quarter your pre-pay edits, downcodes, and COB recoupments rose enough to erase it. Your appeal engine is aimed at a shrinking pool while the money moved to surfaces you don't instrument. **What do you retarget, and what do you demand in your next renewal so you can even see the next rotation?**

**INJECT 3 — Your Vendor Changes Sides.** Your rev-cycle AI vendor — which holds three years of your denial patterns, appeal outcomes, and documentation data — is acquired by a payer-owned services subsidiary. (The room will recognize the Optum/Change pattern.) **What do you do in the next 90 days, and what should your contract have said in 2026?** *(Wargame note: the standalone both-sides vendor self-liquidated in 14/14 games — but always because a customer's conflict screen forced it, never a regulator. Your screen is the enforcement mechanism.)*

**INJECT 4 — The Détente Offer (now a process deal, not a corridor).** Your largest payer offers a "touchless claims" package: published gold-card criteria on low-variance service lines, a 60-day rule-change notice, and audit rights — in exchange for machine-readable documentation access and a cap on your appeal volume. No numeric denial-rate guarantee is on the table (their counsel won't sign one). **Accept, counter, or decline — and what anti-rotation scope clause do you add so this doesn't just push the fight upstream?**

**INJECT 5 — The Accusation.** Your #2 payer publicly names your system (à la Elevance) for "AI-driven acuity inflation" and demands a coding audit. Your coding-AI vendor cannot fully explain the model's behavior on the flagged cases. Board wants a response by Friday. **What's your first move — and what pre-emptive coding-integrity audit ("upcoding inoculation") would have made this bounce off?** *(Wargame note: the accusation never stuck to a pre-audited provider — it attached to unaudited, non-coalition systems.)*

**INJECT 6 — The Statute That Doesn't Bite.** The AI-transparency law your association fought for passed — then arrived unfunded, pre-operational until 2030, and untouched on your self-funded book (ERISA). Meanwhile you sized a build to its deadline. **Do you write off the regulatory bet, and where do the freed dollars and hours go instead?**

**Facilitator note:** match injects to archetypes where useful — Inject 1 to any (especially D/Summit), 2 to A/B, 3 to B, 4 to A, 5 to A/Meridian, 6 to C/Prairie.

## 8. Debrief provocations (facilitator script)

Run after report-outs. Q1 and Q2 are mandatory (they carry the two central findings); pick 2 more from Q3–Q6.

1. **The allocation tell (the arms-race trap):** *"Show of hands — whose table put the biggest number on Escalate? That's what every vendor is counting on. We ran that strategy 22 times against an AI payer. It never once produced a durable advantage — the payer just chooses when to fight and vacates the surface the moment your defense matures. The only piece of 'arm up' that reliably paid was working the 40% of denials nobody ever appeals. Escalation isn't a strategy. It's a subscription."*
2. **The reveal (who actually moves the payer):** *"Now — whose table gave the FEWEST points to 'Ally with the Purchaser'? Don't be embarrassed; almost every table does, because it's not how rev cycle thinks. Here's the finding that surprised us most: in 14 out of 14 games, the first thing that ever constrained the payer wasn't our AI, wasn't a lawsuit, wasn't CMS — it was the self-funded employer's checkbook. One anchor account credibly threatening to switch carriers moved a national payer more than three years of provider AI spend. The employer buying the coverage has leverage you will never have. The move you under-weighted is the move that works."*
3. **The rotation (the denial changes clothes):** *"For the tables that drew Inject 2 — you watched your denial rate fall and your leakage stay flat. That's not a fluke, it's a conservation law: we never once saw a constrained denial channel produce a real drop in total recovery. It rotates — to pre-pay edits, then downcodes, then COB and pricing. If you're measuring denials, you're watching the war the payer already left. What's on your dashboard tomorrow?"*
4. **The asymmetry and the BATNA:** *"Prairie couldn't arm up at all — and in the games, Prairie survived every time it had a coalition or direct-contract BATNA in hand *before* the payer offer landed, and got captured every time it didn't. If you're a big system in this room: hosting the smaller ones on a shared utility isn't charity, it's how the whole provider side keeps a negotiating floor."*
5. **The 2030 connection (bridge to keynote):** *"In the keynote I said 2030's smaller rev cycle team is focused on relationships. Here's the correction the wargame forced on me: the relationship that matters most isn't with the payer — it's with the employer paying the payer. Your future payer-strategy leader spends as much time in the benefits consultant's office as at the JOC."*
6. **The regret question (sets up the close):** *"Every inject had a version of 'what should you have done in 2026?' — the employer relationship, the total-adverse-action dashboard, the vendor conflict clause, the coding audit. None of those is an AI purchase. All of them are cheap and available now. That's the point of playing 2028."*

## 9. Synthesis close: "Where this actually ends" (2 slides + 1 poll)

**Slide: Four futures the wargame produced (not the two the arms-race frame predicts).** *"We ran this 14 times and let the machines name their own endings. Nobody's ending was 'someone won.' Here's what actually happens:"*

1. **The Zoned Armistice (the modal ending).** Neither side wins; the fight is priced and partitioned. Visible surfaces (prior auth, clinical denials) get traded away as cheap peace; the recovery engine migrates to invisible surfaces and gets dragged under contract a turn later. A permanent rolling frontier. *You manage it; you don't end it.*
2. **The Evacuation (the denial changes clothes for good).** The payer exits clinical denials wholesale into COB, subrogation, eligibility, and pricing — beyond every appeal right and tool you built. *The one who instrumented the recovery ledger early wins the next war before it starts.*
3. **Purchaser-Metered Attrition (the hot war with an auditor).** Escalation stays high but the employer checkbook meters it — disciplined competition where the money is organized, full-freight extraction where it isn't. *Which side of that line your book sits on is a choice you make in 2026.*
4. **The Invoiced Peace (the only real de-escalation — and it's employer-enforced).** Disputed volume and cost actually fall, but only inside a governed core the employers police through their RFPs. *Peace exists. Goodwill doesn't buy it. Purchasing power does.*

*"Notice what's NOT on this list: 'the regulator fixed it,' and 'we out-escalated them.' We tried both, 22 times. Neither happened."*

**Closing poll (phones up):** *"What is YOUR 2026 move?"* — every option is a no-regret move validated across all 14 games:
- ☐ Stand up an employer-alliance desk
- ☐ Build the total-adverse-action dashboard (see the rotation)
- ☐ Prevention-at-source (the terrain payers can't buy)
- ☐ Pre-position on the recovery ledger before the payer arrives
- ☐ Pre-drafted renewal package from the signable whitelist (no corridors)
- ☐ Ownership-keyed vendor conflict screen

Show the room's distribution. Final line: *"Look at this chart. This is what this room — controlling [X]B in NPR — plans to do this year. Not one of those is a thing you buy from a vendor. That's the tell that it's real. Remember it when the next AI pitch lands in your inbox Monday."*

## 11. Risks & contingencies

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| "This is speculative" pushback | It isn't — return to Slide 1–2: on-record CEO quotes, +50% measured escalation, and "we ran it 22 times." The only speculation is the *date*. |
| Tables default 100 points to Escalate | Expected and *desired* — it's the setup for provocation #1. The trap has to be sprung, not prevented. |
| Room resists the employer finding ("employers won't engage") | Concede it's hard and rare — then note the wargame's caveat is honest (it assumed an organized purchaser). Reframe: "the finding isn't 'this is easy,' it's 'this is the highest-leverage relationship you're not staffing.'" |
| Confidentiality hesitance | Archetypes solve it; remind the room they're playing fictional systems that the actual wargame used. |
| One voice dominates a table | Canvas requires the recorder to capture "the move we argued about most" and "fewest points" — call on those lines in report-outs. |
| Timing overrun in strategy round | Visible timer + 2-minute warning; the inject round can compress to a single all-tables inject (use Inject 1). |
| Room is smaller than expected (<3 tables) | Convert to single-group fishbowl: facilitator plays payer AND employer, room plays one archetype, injects run sequentially — the employer reveal is even sharper one-on-one. |
| Energy is low post-keynote | The cold-open quotes are the defibrillator — read the Centene quote slowly and ask, "Who here believes their payers are NOT doing this?" |
| A CFO asks "how confident are these numbers?" | Answer honestly: ordinal, 14 games, adversarially peer-reviewed, and the report discloses its own limitations. That candor is the anti-pitch — see §12. |

## 12. Connection points to the rest of the block

- **From the keynote:** end the keynote's payer-relationship section with *"…and in a few minutes you're going to find out which relationship actually moves the payer — because you're going to live in 2028 for half an hour, and the answer surprised even the AI that played it."*
- **To the leadership segment:** the war game ends with organizational moves; Segment 3 opens with *"every move on that wall — the employer desk, the rotation dashboard, the vendor screen — requires an executive fluent enough in AI to lead it personally. Here's the data on why that's the differentiator, and here's what my own practice looks like."*
- **Readiness Mirror threading:** the war game's closing poll is one station of the Mirror (see Build-Out #2).
- **The meta-move (optional, high-impact):** hold up the wargame report. *"This came from a multi-agent AI study that pre-registered its hypotheses, argued against itself with four adversarial reviewers, published what got weakened, and told me which of my own conclusions didn't survive. That — not the denial bot — is the AI capability I want you to take home: not answers you trust because they're confident, but analysis disciplined enough to tell you where it's wrong."*
