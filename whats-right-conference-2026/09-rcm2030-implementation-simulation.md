# The Sierra Health Simulation — Living Through RCM 2030, Day by Day
## What a $4.2B multi-hospital system learns by actually running the 2026–2030 implementation

**Prepared for:** the What's Right In Healthcare working session — operational Rev Cycle SVPs and CFOs
**Method:** A state-threaded, day-level implementation simulation. "Sierra Health" ($4.2B NPR, 9 hospitals, 3 states, one represented market, Epic) executes the full RCM 2030 plan — autonomy ladder with error budgets, staged backfill-freeze waves, triple-duty observation-mode pilot, skills gates, redeployment-not-layoff — from August 2026 through December 2030, in 10 sequential episodes producing a 146-entry dated diary. The plan deliberately embeds everything our prior work already knows (the 2030 POV, the Bridge Builder mechanics, both wargame studies), because the simulation's only job was to find what a *well-advised* plan still cannot see. Honesty rules bound every episode: metrics move slowly and sometimes backwards, savings lag plan, some failures stay failed, and a third of events must go well — with the reason why. Two adversarial reviewers — a 30-year health-system COO and a unionized-healthcare HR/change executive — then killed or corrected every lesson; nothing survived to this report on simulation evidence alone.
**How to read this:** the events are simulated; the mechanisms are real. Every lesson names the early indicator that reveals it and the move that pre-empts it. The reviewers' own additions — things even the simulation missed — are disclosed at the end.

# 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Bottom line: the agents were the most dependable part of the program. Sierra closed 2030 at $27.2M of verified savings against a $31.5M plan — about 80 cents on the modeled dollar — with breakeven restated to mid-2032 and the human floor restated from 70 to 85 FTE. Both reviewers, an operator and a labor-side practitioner, called that the good outcome. Nothing that nearly killed the program was a model failure. It was calendars, contracts, labor mechanics, and measurement plumbing — none of it on the original risk register, all of it discoverable only by running the collisions.

The single most transferable engineering lesson survived both reviews untouched: promote autonomy on verifiability, not error history. The class that went to full autonomy cleanly was the one where every action reconciled against 835/portal ground truth within 48 hours. The $412K breach ran 13 days undetected in a class that HAD human approval. Error rate tells you how often the agent fails; verifiability tells you how long a failure compounds unseen. Rank the roadmap by that, and accept that appeal drafting stays low-autonomy forever — there is no ground truth for an argument.

What planning cannot teach is that the damage comes from joins between independently correct decisions. The freeze wave that banked savings removed the exact labor class the next autonomy tier consumed. The close-week blackout — itself a fix — read as model drift. The retention tranches — a sensible fix for a synchronized exit — published a departure calendar recruiters priced to the day. Every workstream was right in isolation. Assign an owner to the joins: the autonomy ladder's labor bill against the headcount calendar, the AP invoice trend against pod volume, timely-filing runways against every wave.

The money story is a translation problem. Avoided cost does not exist in the GL; the program was suspended for four weeks by a new CFO doing ordinary variance analysis, with $8.3M of claimed savings unlocatable. The fix that held was a cost-center spine and a monthly GL-tied ledger — co-signed by FP&A, per the operator reviewer, or the successor CFO discounts it as advocacy — with cash acceleration separated from recurring savings. Budget four permanent lines the pro forma had at zero: supervision labor at 15-25% of displaced cost, forever; the overpayment refund conveyor the same logs create under the 60-day rule; provenance and audit defense at 1.5-2% of gross savings; and the raised case-mix floor.

The people story was corrected hardest in review, in both directions. The union was the program's best design partner — bargaining forced grading to be paid, scheduled, coded, and quota-relieved, and that design became the standard; anything not in the timekeeping system does not exist. But the labor reviewer added the exposure the simulation underpriced: gate scores used for selection are a self-documenting adverse-impact and arbitration case unless validated as job-related and custodied in your own systems, and shadow offshore work in a represented shop is a seven-figure subcontracting grievance, not a metric-optics problem. Retention that held was scope — title, team, second seat — not dated cash.

On the payer: the reviewers replaced the chess-master with something worse. Payers don't need to reverse-engineer your governance; they generate friction — downcodes, pends, record requests — at near-zero marginal cost while your response costs real money, and the dollars migrate into whatever category and cost center your program doesn't own. The denial rate improved partly because the battlefield left it. Charter full-waterfall yield defense under one executive, and treat the appeals arms race as unwinnable on unit economics — the durable levers are prevention and contract terms.

Two honest caveats from review. First, the simulation assumed a control over workforce flow that labor-scarce reality may not grant — many systems automate because they cannot staff the work, which makes savings even harder to prove. Second, it never priced an external catastrophe: a clearinghouse outage or payer portal credential lockout of the Change Healthcare class would have hit an automated, atrophied-fallback operation harder than anything simulated. Run that drill anyway.

# 2. WHAT THESE EXECUTIVES AREN'T THINKING OF — THE ELEVEN

## 1. Verifiability, not accuracy, decides what gets autonomy
**The clean full-autonomy class was machine-checkable against 835 ground truth in 48 hours; the $412K breach ran 13 undetected days in a class WITH human approval.**

Error rate measures how often an agent fails; verifiability measures how long a failure compounds unseen. Human approval is a weak, decaying control (see the rubber-stamping finding); fast reconciliation against remittance or portal ground truth is a strong one that does not decay. Both reviewers confirmed this without correction — the operator called it the best engineering lesson in the set. It also explains why cash posting and status work are safe to automate and appeal drafting never is: there is no ground-truth reconciliation for an argument.

- **Early indicator:** For every candidate action class, ask one question before any pilot: if the agent does this wrong, what tells us, and how many hours later? If the answer is 'a human notices' or 'the next audit,' the class is not promotion-eligible.
- **The move:** No autonomy promotion without a measured mean-time-to-detect from seeded errors. Rank the entire automation roadmap by verifiability of the action class before ROI, before volume, before anything.

## 2. Your people's graded judgments are crown-jewel IP walking out through the vendor contract
**610,000 human-graded appeal decisions ended up owned-adjacent to the payer side after two vendor acquisitions; the firewall Sierra got was prospective-only.**

The corpus is exhaust from a QA design, so it appears on no asset register and no diligence checklist. The operator reviewer's correction makes it worse: the leak path is rarely the dramatic acquisition — it is the 'de-identified aggregate analytics' clause already in the contract you signed, which the M&A merely repoints at your adversary. The labor reviewer adds that vendor custody also means you cannot answer a union information request, a grievance subpoena, or discovery about employment decisions made on that data.

- **Early indicator:** One question, answerable in week one of vendor negotiation: who owns, and who may train on, the human-graded outputs after a change of control? If the answer takes more than a sentence, the gap exists.
- **The move:** In the first SOW, while you still have signing leverage: customer ownership of graded outputs, no cross-tenant training, certified deletion at change of control, customer non-solicit — and a rule that any data used in an employment decision lives in a system you custody under your retention schedule.

## 3. Human-in-the-loop without decoys is a ritual, not a control
**During close week, approvers rubber-stamped 7 of 8 seeded bad actions at 11-second dwell while the SLA dashboard showed 99.4% green.**

Every dashboard measures throughput; none measures attention, and the failure mode produces MORE green, faster. The role also adverse-selects: any duty that earns no gate credit or comp gets staffed by whoever can be spared — by definition your bottom quartile. The operator's correction is the sharpest part: the simulation only knew because it seeded decoys, and real shops never do, so the collapse is genuinely invisible until a payer audit two years later. The labor reviewer adds that in a represented shop, covert decoy-seeding against staff draws a surveillance grievance — negotiate the protocol openly, scoring the system, not individuals.

- **Early indicator:** Approval dwell-time distribution compressing toward the UI minimum — visible within days of any load spike — and approver volunteer lists skewing low on QA history.
- **The move:** Randomized decoys with attention scoring from the first hour of production autonomy; dwell floors and per-approver caps; pause autonomous execution during close weeks; make approval duty carry comp and gate credit before go-live. Budget the ~15% throughput haircut as the permanent price of a real control.

## 4. The automation consumes a labor class your own glide path eliminates on schedule
**Supervised autonomy cost ~22 analyst-minutes per agent-hour, and the freeze wave removed exactly those analysts in exactly the go-live quarter — $740K of appeal rights expired in one such collision, permanently.**

The freeze schedule and the supervision design were authored by different workstreams, each correct in isolation; nothing joined the autonomy ladder's labor bill to the headcount calendar. The operator sharpened the unit: what actually bites is exception rate times handling time, arriving in payer-driven bursts — a policy change turns 2% exceptions into 30% overnight. The labor reviewer adds that recall and bumping rights mean the bodies who return after a reversal are not the qualified ones who left; denominate in qualified people, not FTEs.

- **Early indicator:** Multiply supervision-minutes-per-agent-hour from observation telemetry by the promotion roadmap BEFORE ratifying any freeze wave. If the product exceeds surviving capacity, the collision is already scheduled. Run a timely-filing runway report at every wave meeting.
- **The move:** Put supervision and exception labor in the board deck as a permanent line at 15-25% of displaced labor cost, priced before approval — retrofitting it as a series of variance findings is what kills sponsorship.

## 5. Your rollback plan reverts onto a workforce that no longer exists
**When the breach auto-demoted a class mid-close, 'revert to manual workqueue' met a queue whose humans had been frozen and redeployed a year earlier: 9-day backlog, +1.2 AR days, contractors needing weeks of training.**

Demotion tabletops end at 'revert to manual' without naming humans, current system access, or FTE-days, so the fallback is fictional and nothing tests it. The operator reviewer pointed at Change Healthcare, February 2024, as this failure at national scale — the manual muscle memory, the people, and even the access were gone. The recurrence is worse: float-pool certifications silently lapsed during a throttle, so the contingency mechanism was unavailable the day the retention cliff hit. Both reviewers corrected the recovery estimate upward: 4-6 weeks to safe productivity including provisioning, not two.

- **Early indicator:** A demotion runbook with no staffing line for the reverted state, and no certification-currency expiry dashboard for contingency staff.
- **The move:** Staff a permanent fallback bench sized to the largest single agent class, give contingency staff protected timekept grading allotments — and run quarterly live-fire drills where the fallback humans actually work the queue. If the drill has never run, the fallback does not exist.

## 6. The logs that find underpayments find overpayments — and identification starts a legal clock and builds a relator's case file
**The celebrated $2.3M underpayment find had a symmetric twin: $1.4M of refunds under the 60-day rule, budgeted nowhere, a permanent conveyor scaling with agent coverage.**

Nobody asks the symmetric question because the answer costs money. Under ACA 6402(a), an identified overpayment not refunded in 60 days becomes a reverse false claim; the operator's calibration is that a reasonable-diligence quantification period exists, but the obligation attaches once your own logs flag it — you cannot un-know it. The labor reviewer added the sleeper: your analysts see those flags. Leadership visibly sitting on overpayment evidence manufactures qui tam relators from your own workforce, with the disagreement logs as exhibit A. The same applies to the affirmative side: an agent applying one wrong rule generates thousands of identical claims with treble-damage exposure.

- **Early indicator:** The day you book the first underpayment find, demand the overpayment count from the same logs. If no one can produce it, the conveyor is running unmanaged.
- **The move:** A compliance-owned refund workflow with counsel privilege sorted before observation mode starts; a contra-revenue line in the pro forma scaling with coverage; and visible internal disposition of every flag — an employee who watches flagged overpayments get worked has no case; one who watches them get buried has both.

## 7. The payer does not fight your program — it exits your taxonomy
**When agents constrained clinical denials, the dollars reappeared as DRG downcodes in HIM/CDI ($6.8M/yr) and COB pends ($2.3M/month) — while every program dashboard improved.**

A downcoded claim PAID — it is just short — so it never appears on a denial dashboard, and the program's KPI improves while net yield bleeds through doors it does not watch. The reviewers replaced the simulation's chess-master payer with the real adversary: payers respond to provider effectiveness with cheap, blunt volume — more downcodes, pends, record requests — because their marginal cost of friction is near zero and yours is not. That asymmetry, not counter-intelligence, is the strategic problem, and it also means an appeals arms race is unwinnable on unit economics. What survived from the original: appeal fingerprinting and sharding policy changes under negotiated disclosure thresholds are real today — the binding term is the aggregation clause, not the threshold.

- **Early indicator:** A standing weekly pended/suspended-dollars-by-payer report next to the denial dashboard (the pre-built version caught the second rotation in 3 weeks versus 8 months), and any program KPI improving while yield per case-mix-adjusted discharge stalls.
- **The move:** Charter the program as full-waterfall yield defense under a single executive who owns net revenue yield across denials, HIM/CDI, and access — and put the durable dollars into prevention and contract terms, not appeal throughput. Note the labor overlay: extending the program into CDI means a new bargaining unit and fresh effects bargaining, which is why full-waterfall must be chartered on day one.

## 8. Your savings are invisible in the general ledger, and the program dies at the first CFO transition
**A new CFO running ordinary variance analysis found labor down $9.1M, purchased services up $6.3M, no bridge — and rationally suspended the program with $8.3M of claimed savings unlocatable.**

The program reports in run-rate currency; finance closes in GL currency; nothing forces reconciliation until the sponsor change removes the person carrying the translation in their head. Compounding it: finance banked $1.4M of pre-existing vacancy as day-one savings and built out-year targets off a two-week honeymoon via live dashboard access — a ratchet the program never escaped. The operator added two corrections: separate one-time cash acceleration (AR days) from recurring savings or finance double-counts it, and the ledger must be co-signed by the CFO's own FP&A team or the successor discounts it as advocacy. The labor reviewer added the own goal: the union's researchers read the same board decks — you cannot claim $9.1M of labor savings upstairs and plead poverty at the table.

- **Early indicator:** The controller's monthly variance commentary never citing the program's run-rate deck, from quarter one. And Finance requesting live dashboard access — the request itself is the warning.
- **The move:** Build the cost-center spine and a monthly GL-tied, FP&A-co-signed savings ledger before the first dollar moves; pre-agree in writing which savings are program versus vacancy; give Finance certified snapshots, not live dashboards; keep the incident-cost receipts continuously — the one-page avoided-breach comparison is what saves the safety budget later.

## 9. Competency gates used for selection are self-documenting legal exposure and a capability monoculture
**Scores logged for promotion decisions, sitting in vendor custody, unvalidated for job-relatedness, in a program whose endpoint is headcount reduction — the discovery trail writes itself.**

Both reviewers weakened the original monoculture claim: gate-passed specialists do not revert to lay baseline when the war rotates, but agreement-threshold gates do select for conformity (inter-grader agreement above ~95% measures homogeneity, not skill), and payer rotation moves the fight across discipline boundaries — DRG defense is a different profession, so the answer is make-vs-buy triggers for new skill classes, not curriculum refreshes. The labor reviewer added what the simulation missed entirely: a gate feeding promotion and selection must be validated as job-related; pass rates broken out by age, race, and sex will show adverse impact somewhere, and new-tech gates reliably skew against older workers. One adverse arbitration award — a 12-24 month clock — can void the gate mechanism and unwind promotions mid-glide.

- **Early indicator:** Inter-grader agreement climbing above ~95%; gate content unchanged across documented payer rotations; any promotion dossier citing vendor-hosted records as its evidence base; no adverse-impact monitoring in place.
- **The move:** Validate gates as job-related before the first selection decision, monitor pass-rate demographics continuously, custody every score in your own system of record, add a calibrated-disagreement track — and carry arbitration outcomes as a program risk line next to the payer.

## 10. The human floor is a case-mix floor, and cost cutters will delete your burnout margin as found savings
**Automating everything automatable left residual queues 100% hardest-case: time-per-case went 14 to 51 minutes, the top performer went out on FMLA, and the honest floor was 85, not the modeled 70.**

Workforce models price average productivity; none price what the job becomes for the people who remain. The survivable fix — retaining ~15% automatable recovery work in mixed queues — permanently reduces terminal savings and doubles as the surge-flex buffer (sell it as that, per the operator, or it loses the CFO argument). The floor also fails stochastically before structurally: a nine-person gated queue is one FMLA plus one jury summons from missing coverage. Reviewer corrections: the real early signals are sick-call rates, FMLA incidence, and exit themes, not an occupational-health flag; productivity standards must be case-mix-adjusted the same quarter automation lands, or your own standards do the payer's work; and the absence-model lambda rises every freeze year as the never-refreshed workforce ages.

- **Early indicator:** Average handled-case time and difficulty mix per analyst, trackable from grading data from day one — it rises every quarter the automation works as designed. Same-day emergency queue reassignments per month for small-queue variance.
- **The move:** Automate to the burnout frontier, not the technical frontier: protect the retained recovery work explicitly in job-design documentation and the pro forma — because the first cost review will find 'automatable work done by humans' and delete it — and express the floor as minimum effective coverage per gated capability with an absence-variance margin.

## 11. Audit the ruler with the same rigor as the model — your measurement substrate lies, and it marks named humans
**Stale snapshots manufactured ~23% false disagreements, a portal redesign was billed as $38K of agent error, close weeks silently ate 3 of 13 grading weeks from every gate clock, and annual error budgets froze promotions every December.**

Evidence-based gates create total trust in the evidence pipeline, and every distortion was generated by a CORRECT safety or finance decision, so nobody audits it. The operator's correction: the bias is not reliably pessimistic — stale data also hides errors — so the lesson is direction-agnostic. The labor reviewer's addition is the one CFOs miss: every false disagreement is a mark on a named person's score in a selection system; a grievance that surfaces the false-disagreement rate taints every promotion made on the data, and the arbitral remedy is re-running gates. The sandbagging finding is pure incentive design: pod owners freezing promotions rather than owning a December breach is rational behavior under an annual budget.

- **Early indicator:** Ask in architecture review: what is the maximum age of the data the agent is graded on, and does the rubric distinguish model error from world drift? Plot promotion submissions against error-budget consumption — the freeze appears at ~60% spend.
- **The move:** Set a data-latency budget reconciled against the grading rubric; ratify attribution rules (model / world drift / approval failure) with a neutral adjudicator before dollars or careers attach; denominate gate clocks in grading-eligible weeks; allocate error budgets per pod per quarter; version-stamp which measurement regime each person's scores were earned under.

# 3. THE METRICS STORY (what "success" actually looked like)

The headline metrics tell a true story only after three restatements. The initial denial rate began as fiction: 11.4% board-approved, 12.1% honest once the offshore write-off laundering was found (Sep 2026), ending at 9.5% — but the ending number flatters, because by 2029–30 the payer war had rotated into DRG downcodes, COB pends, and lookback disputes that the denial taxonomy structurally cannot see; the denial rate improved partly because the battlefield left it. AR days went the wrong way for three years (48.2 → 53.8 at the April 2028 breach trough) before recovering to 50.3, absorbing every collision the plan created — freeze holes, payer slow-lanes, demotion backlogs, COB leakage. Savings ran behind plan every single half, never once crossing it: $1.4M of the first 'savings' was banked pre-existing vacancy, the 2027–28 targets were ratcheted off that banking and a two-week T2 honeymoon, and the program closed 2030 at $27.2M against $31.5M — roughly 80 cents on the modeled dollar, with the honest gap driven by four permanent unmodeled cost lines: approver/safety labor (~15% throughput haircut plus bench and reserve), the overpayment refund conveyor, provenance defense (~1.5–2% of gross savings), and the raised human floor (85 vs 70, a case-mix burnout reality, not a negotiation). Pod FTE hit 121 against a modeled 105 — but the last cut was made by Meridian's recruiters and the tranche cliff, not by the glide, and 9,400 accounts/month of shadow offshore work meant the FTE number was never the real labor number anyway; the metric that survives is total labor cost per resolved claim, adopted under duress in June 2030. Attrition never improved (19% baseline, 22% at close, persistently concentrated in the trained and credentialed). Cash breakeven was declared for 2030 and restated to mid-2032. What was declared vs real, in one line: the program declared a denial-rate victory and an FTE glide; what it actually built was a governance machine, an honest cost basis, and a payer-intelligence capability — worth having, two years later and 20% smaller than the deck promised, and only countable at all because Whitfield refused, four separate times (baseline restatement, Cascade Blue one-time booking, refund transparency, breakeven restatement), to let a flattering number stand.

# 6. WHAT WENT RIGHT — and exactly why (the ahas)

- Observation mode has standalone economic value the business case scored at zero: read-only agents watching all pods became a payer-behavior radar (caught Vantara's prior-auth change 18 hours before any human read the bulletin, E2 Feb 2027; detected the July 2027 portal redesign days before denial reports moved) and a contract-variance detector (the $2.3M Cascade Blue underpayment, E2 Mar 2027). Ask in every value review: 'what is the observation phase worth if we never promote a single agent?' A zero on that line means cross-entity pattern detection was never priced.
- Promote autonomy on verifiability, not error history. The class that went to T3 cleanly (Mar 2028) was the one where every action was machine-checkable against 835/portal ground truth within 48 hours — while the April 2028 breach ($412K in 13 undetected days) happened in a T2 class WITH human approval. Mean-time-to-detect a seeded error dominates supervision tier; error rate tells you how often the agent fails, verifiability tells you how long a failure compounds unseen.
- The transferable asset in multi-site scale is the control layer, not the agent: Saint Clare reached T1-live in 4 weeks vs 11 for the pilot site (Apr 2028) because the decoy program, traveling approver bench, and pre-ratified caps arrived day one while agent config took 3 days. Budget scale-out as a safety-culture transplant with a human bench line item, not a software deployment.
- Adversarial process is a design-quality mechanism. The represented site — treated as the schedule risk — produced the only clean grading corpus (E2), because bargaining forced grading to be paid, scheduled, coded, quota-relieved; that design became the system standard. General law: funded/scheduled/coded beats 'protected' every time (Kronos pay code took training attendance 40%→85% in one week).
- Pre-committed, boring governance beats reactive governance: the seasonal autonomy calendar handled the Oct 2030 flu surge (108% census) with zero emergency intervention because the step-down was decided months earlier outside the panic window — vs the 2029 throttle chaos. Bonus synergy worth designing on purpose: the burnout fix (retained easy work in mixed queues) turned out to be the surge-flex buffer.
- Moment-of-action provenance logging built for the error cap doubled as the audit defense: the SMRC demand was answered in 9 days with full decision lineage (Jan 2029), the package became the template for payer reciprocal audits, and it flipped a CMS-adjacent threat into a board trust asset. Safety plumbing and audit posture are one investment, not two.
- Honoring safety mechanisms at visible public cost is what converts them into currency: refusing early re-promotion through a painful close week (Apr–Jun 2028), enforcing the Riverbend gate against the sponsor's own board condition (Feb 2028), and unprompted incident disclosures bought the union LOA, the board expansion vote, and the activist director's conversion. Governance trust compounds only when the org pays the price in public.
- Owned instrumentation beats negotiated language: the conceded aggregation clause did nothing against Cascade Blue's nine sharded sub-threshold edits; the in-housed $410K edit-telemetry pipeline's release fingerprints won the $1.6M dispute in one JOC session (Aug 2029). Agent telemetry also delivered Sierra's first process-term payer contract in history — leverage no denial-rate corridor ever produced.
- Role-based retention (title, team, second-seat analyst, publication rights) held people that cash could not: Ortega stayed where nine tranche-holders walked, and the tranche-2 conversion landed 14 of 20 at ~60 cents on the vest dollar (Aug 2030). Scope is harder to counter-offer than money.

# 11. PEER REVIEW — what was killed, corrected, and added

No item was refuted by both reviewers, so nothing was dropped outright — but four items were weakened and appear here only in corrected form, and the confirmed items absorbed substantial floor-level corrections. (1) The payer-as-adversarial-engineer thread was cut down hardest: the operator ruled the engineered error-budget flood and authorship-attestation demands over-fit (payers don't know your internal governance mechanics and don't need to), and replaced the chess-master with the real strategic problem — near-zero-marginal-cost friction asymmetry. What survived: appeal fingerprinting, threshold-sharding (fight the aggregation clause, not the number), and de facto slow lanes visible in 835 data. The labor reviewer's salvage — attestation demands as a workforce/licensure crisis needing a counsel-drafted policy — was retained in the people material. (2) The '0 of 47 above untrained baseline' monoculture finding was rejected as to magnitude by both reviewers on different grounds (discipline boundary, not training decay; experienced humans degrade to a 12-18 month gap, not zero) and was restated as two lessons: agreement >95% as a conformity warning, and make-vs-buy triggers for whole new skill classes — plus the labor reviewer's added legal-validation requirement, which was promoted into the top-10 because the evidence (logged scores driving selection in a headcount-reduction program) fully supports it. (3) The observation-mode aha was demoted: contract-variance underpayment detection is a 30-year-old product category, so the standalone value was restated as payer behavior-change radar, priced net of what competent contract management already delivers — it appears in the executive summary, not the top 10. (4) The shadow-outsourcing discovery story was corrected for represented shops: 9,400 accounts/month of unit work does not hide until a vendor renewal — it surfaces as a subcontracting grievance with back-pay exposure within weeks; the total-labor-cost denomination fix survives. Confirmed items were tightened per review: decoy programs reframed from detection tool to the thing that makes review a control at all; Epic retraining 4-6 weeks, not two, plus quarterly live-fire drills; the 60-day clock given its diligence period but extended with qui tam/relator risk; measurement bias made direction-agnostic; the savings ledger made FP&A-co-signed with cash acceleration split from recurring. Reviewer-added lessons incorporated where the simulation's own evidence supports them: gate legal exposure, arbitration clock, score custody, workforce-aging lambda, union-readable board decks. Reviewer-added items outside the simulation's evidence — labor scarcity inverting the glide premise, clearinghouse/portal utility failure, Epic platform risk, vendor delivery decay, program-leader succession — are flagged honestly as blind spots of the simulation itself in the executive summary rather than dressed up as simulated findings.

**Lessons the reviewers say even this simulation missed (disclosed, not simulated):**
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Labor scarcity, not labor surplus, is the actual 2024-2026 revenue-cycle reality — and it inverts the whole glide-path premise. Billing and follow-up roles run 20-40% annual turnover with months-long time-to-fill; most real systems automate because they CANNOT staff the work, not to harvest FTE savings. A simulation built around carefully sequenced freeze waves and forced redeployment assumes a control over workforce flow that no rev-cycle VP has had in a decade. The real program's savings story is 'absorbed volume growth without hiring,' which is even harder to prove in a GL than avoided cost.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Payer portal terms of service and credential lockout: payers and intermediaries (Availity et al.) contractually prohibit bots and actively detect automated portal traffic, and they can and do cut off provider portal credentials. Losing portal access for a major payer is an existential operational event that no amount of internal governance mitigates. Any agent architecture that touches payer portals is running on the adversary's infrastructure at the adversary's pleasure — the simulation has payers fingerprinting appeal templates but misses the far cruder, already-real lever of simply banning the bots.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Epic platform risk in both directions: (1) every quarterly Epic upgrade breaks screen-scraping and workqueue automation, making the EHR release calendar a permanent tax on the agent fleet (the simulation touches a change-calendar collision once but misses the chronic version); (2) Epic ships competing native functionality mid-program — payer platform, denial workflows, its own AI roadmap — which can strand the third-party vendor stack and its ROI case overnight. The build-on-vendor vs wait-for-Epic decision recurs every budget cycle and the simulation never once forces it.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* The clearinghouse/utility single point of failure: Change Healthcare (Feb 2024) took down claim submission, remits, and eligibility for a large fraction of US providers for weeks. A four-year simulation of rev-cycle automation with no external infrastructure catastrophe — no clearinghouse outage, no ransomware on the health system itself, no downtime procedures test — missed the single largest rev-cycle operational event of the decade. Automated pipelines make you MORE brittle to utility failure because the manual fallback atrophied (the simulation derives this for its own demotions but never from external outage).
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Affirmative False Claims Act exposure and the qui tam pipeline you are building: an agent that systematically applies a wrong billing rule generates thousands of identical false claims with treble damages and per-claim penalties, and statistical sampling makes extrapolation easy for the government. Worse, a multi-year program that displaces and embitters hundreds of billing staff — people who know exactly where the bodies are and have seen the disagreement logs — is a whistleblower generation machine. The simulation covers reverse false claims (overpayments) but never the affirmative exposure or the relator risk from its own glide path.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Patient-facing blast radius: one bad automated batch touching patient statements, balance billing, charity-care screening (501(r)), or collections referrals produces attorney-general complaints, local press, and board fury out of all proportion to dollars — and state medical-debt and credit-reporting law is tightening yearly. The simulation's adversary is entirely the payer; in reality the fastest way an automation program dies is a patient-harm story, and rev-cycle agents sit adjacent to patient billing at every turn.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Middle management is the layer that decides whether any of this happens, and it's the layer the savings math targets. Supervisors and managers whose spans shrink slow-walk implementations, hoard exception work to justify headcount, and control the local data the program depends on. The simulation has one position-control mechanic but never the political economy: manager-layer savings are always in the pro forma and never realized, and unmanaged manager resistance adds quarters to every wave.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Denials are mostly manufactured upstream of the revenue cycle — physician documentation, utilization review, prior-auth workflow, front-end registration quality. An appeal-automation program hits a plateau determined by clinical operations it does not own, and the highest-ROI moves (denial PREVENTION, clean-claim edits, CDI) compete for the same capital. The deeper strategic miss: appeals are an arms race you lose on unit economics, because the payer generates denials at near-zero marginal cost while you appeal at positive cost even with agents. The durable lever is payer contract terms and prevention, and the simulation only discovers contract leverage as a late-game aha.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Vendor delivery-quality decay and the pilot-to-production cliff: the A-team that ran your pilot is on someone else's pilot within two quarters of signature; model performance quietly degrades as the vendor rebalances capacity across tenants; roadmap promises slip. The simulation's vendor risk is all exotic (M&A, IP, poaching) and none of it is the mundane version every operator has actually lived — the account team churns and the product stops improving while the invoices keep arriving.
- *(30-year health-system COO)* Program leadership succession on the provider side: a four-to-five-year transformation outlives its operational champions, not just its CFO. The VP of revenue cycle who built the governance trust, the union relationships, and the payer JOC credibility leaves in year 2-3 (the market for people who have done this triples their price — the simulation even builds that market via Meridian and never notices its own program director is the most poachable asset). Succession depth for program leadership, not just approver benches, is a missing line in every workforce exhibit.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* The CBA expires mid-program and nothing in the simulation survives contact with a successor-contract negotiation: a four-year automation glide path crossing a 2028-29 contract renegotiation will have its gates, scores, autonomy tiers, and freeze waves bargained as chips — or become a strike issue. Negotiate a standalone technology LOA with its own defined reopener early, so the program's terms don't ride into (and get repriced by) full contract negotiations.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* The union-as-design-partner needs a vehicle, not a sentiment: a negotiated joint labor-management technology committee with union access to the telemetry, decoy protocols, and error-attribution decisions — ideally a union-nominated auditor. Without its own window into the data, union trust collapses at the first incident regardless of how early you bargained.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* The stranded-incumbent class is priced nowhere: 20-40% of incumbents will never pass the gates, and CBA job security means you cannot exit them. You accumulate a red-circled, wage-protected population doing vanishing work — redeployment, retraining, and carry costs that belong in the glide-path pro forma from day one.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* Employment-law exposure of algorithmic scoring is entirely absent: gate pass rates and grading scores broken out by age, race, and sex will show adverse impact somewhere, in a program whose explicit endpoint is workforce reduction — ADEA disparate-impact exposure with a self-documenting discovery trail, plus AI-in-employment statutes (bias audits, notice, appeal rights) that directly regulate this scoring by the program's mid-years. Validation studies and adverse-impact monitoring are program infrastructure, not HR overhead.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* The grievance-arbitration clock is a program risk on par with the payer: a contested selection or discipline decision takes 12-24 months to arbitrate, and one adverse award can void the gate mechanism, order reinstatement and back pay, and retroactively unwind promotions mid-glide. Design gates to be arbitrally defensible — validated, transparent, appealable — not merely accurate, and carry arbitration outcomes as a program risk line.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* First-line supervisors are the most threatened class and the actual implementers, and the simulation never breaks them: expect exception-work hoarding, refusal to release stars to grading and approval duty, and quiet sabotage of telemetry that argues for their own elimination. The position-control demotion in the calendar is the comp symptom; the behavioral risk — supervisor resistance and attrition — is a top-three failure mode in every real transition I have run, and it needs an explicit supervisor career path through the end state.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* Attrition assumptions fail in both directions after every wave announcement (survivor syndrome): the people who can leave — your gate-track stars — leave early and un-modeled, while strong-benefit health systems get 'stuck' incumbents in exactly the classes being shed. Regretted-attrition spikes follow announcements, not effective dates; model announcement-triggered attrition, not just scheduled reductions.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* Pay compression cascades from every new differential: approver premiums and grader stipends compress against supervisors and adjacent classifications, generating me-too grievances and forcing a comp-structure rebuild in year two — a predictable cost the moment you monetize any new duty, and doubly so in a represented market where every differential is visible and bargainable.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* Freeze-driven workforce aging makes the 'ordinary bad week' progressively less ordinary: four years of no hiring into a class raises average age and tenure, and FMLA, LOA, and absence incidence trend up with it — the Sep 2029 stochastic floor failure is a drifting distribution, not bad luck. The Poisson absence model needs a lambda that rises every freeze year.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* None of the simulated failures are story-shaped, but the real near-death event usually is: one viral internal narrative — a beloved 20-year employee demoted by the algorithm, a grading score surfacing in a termination — can freeze executive support regardless of green dashboards. Case-by-case human review of any adverse employment action touching program data, and a standing communication discipline, are program infrastructure.
- *(unionized-healthcare HR/change executive)* Severance, release, and bumping economics never appear: even an attrition-led glide ends with involuntary separations somewhere, and a represented RIF triggers cross-site bumping cascades (whose disruption cost exceeds the severance), CBA severance schedules, potential WARN aggregation across waves, and releases you will want to trade for knowledge transfer from exactly the people whose judgment the corpus encodes.
